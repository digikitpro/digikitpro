How to Paint Eyes in Procreate: A Step-by-Step Guide for Beginners

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Eyes are the feature that decides whether a portrait feels alive. Viewers look at eyes first, stay there longest, and judge the whole face from them — which is why beginners freeze here. The good news is that a believable eye is not a mystery. It is a small, repeatable process with six steps, and once you have done it a dozen times it becomes as automatic as a signature.

This guide walks through the full process in Procreate: the shape, the base colours, the iris, the pupil and its reflections, the shadow that gives the eye its depth, and the finishing details. It assumes you have a portrait in progress or are starting a head study — the full portrait workflow guide shows where eyes sit in the overall sequence.

Before you start: setup that saves the painting

Three minutes of setup prevents ninety percent of eye-painting pain:

  • Reference, always. A real photograph of an eye, ideally lit from the direction your portrait's light comes from. You do not need to copy it — you need its logic: where the light hits, where the shadow falls, how dark the shadow really is.
  • A dedicated canvas area or a fresh study file. Practising eyes on a small canvas (a 2000×2500 pixel practice setup is plenty) is faster to repeat than practising on a full portrait.
  • Separate layers for each part. Sclera, iris, pupil, shadow, and lashes on their own layers. The full setup is in the table near the end of this article. Layers cost nothing and make every correction non-destructive.

Step 1 — Draw the shape

The eye is two curves, not one almond. Draw the upper lid and the lower lid as separate lines:

  • The upper lid is the stronger curve — it carries more of the shape's roundness and sits higher above the iris.
  • The lower lid is flatter, and it usually does not touch the iris at all: there is a gap of sclera between the lower lid and the iris in most reference photos. Beginners close that gap and the eye reads as heavy or sleepy.
  • The inner corner (tear duct side) is lower, pointed, and often covered by a small fold. The outer corner is higher and rounder.

Keep the line light — a sketch brush at low opacity — because you will adjust the shape as the iris goes in. The eye sits on the horizontal midline of the head; if your construction has an eye line, the lids straddle it with the iris centred below it.

Step 2 — Base colours: the sclera is not white

This is the fix for most "off" eyes. The white of the eye — the sclera — is not white. It carries the skin's underlying warmth and the colour of the ambient light. In most portraits it is a warm grey or a soft blue-grey, a few steps down from white, and it is never the same value across the whole shape:

  • Lighter where the light hits directly (usually the upper area, near the upper lid).
  • Warmer and slightly darker toward the inner corner, where the caruncle (the small pink fold) sits.
  • Shadowed under the upper lid — a soft band of shadow across the top of the eye, more on it later.

Paint the sclera as one soft base with a clean brush, then add those three variations with a soft blender at low opacity. Resist the urge to make it white "so the iris pops" — a slightly grey sclera makes the iris read as sitting inside a socket, which is the whole point.

Step 3 — Paint the iris: radial, not flat

The iris is a small dome, not a sticker. Three facts make it read as curved:

  1. The outer edge is darker. The limbal ring — a darker circle around the iris where it meets the sclera — is real anatomy, not a style choice. Paint it as a soft dark ring, maybe 20–30% darker than the iris base, blending inward. It is the single highest-impact detail in a small eye.
  2. The light hits one side. Wherever your light source is, that part of the iris is lighter and the opposite part is darker. In the most common top-light setup, the bottom of the iris is darker than the top, and the shadow of the upper lid will deepen it further.
  3. The strokes follow the pupil. Real irises are full of fine radial texture. You do not need to draw individual fibres — you need the direction: short strokes radiating out from the pupil, lighter toward the lit side, breaking up the flat base. A small textured brush or a fine liner at low opacity, a dozen strokes, is enough at most sizes.

Order: iris base colour (a mid value, a touch darker than the sclera's grey), then the limbal ring, then the light/dark split, then the radial strokes. Each one on its own layer if you want to rework without repainting.

Step 4 — Pupil and the reflections that make it alive

The pupil is the darkest thing in the eye — darker than any shadow you have painted so far — and it is smaller than beginners expect. If the pupil swallows the iris, the eye reads as alien. As a check: in most adult eyes the iris is roughly three to four times the pupil's diameter.

Now the reflections, in order of importance:

  • The primary catchlight. A small, bright highlight on the pupil, positioned exactly where the light source is — top left if the light is top left, in both eyes, at the same height. One clean shape. This is the detail viewers subconsciously check for "life."
  • The secondary reflection. A much smaller, softer light at the opposite edge of the pupil or lower iris — light bouncing back from the face. Opacity 30–40%. It is the difference between "lit" and "alive."
  • The iris reflection. A faint lighter zone on the iris below the catchlight, the same light wrapping around the dome. Keep it soft.

Keep the catchlights on their own layer so you can reposition them if the rest of the portrait's light source shifts later. A catchlight that disagrees with the nose shadow is the fastest way to make a face look "wrong" without you knowing why.

Step 5 — The shadow that gives the eye depth

Two shadows do most of the work here, and both come from the face's own geometry:

  • The upper-lid shadow. The eyelid overhangs the eye, so it casts a soft shadow across the top of the iris and the upper sclera. Paint it as a soft band, darker than the sclera, fading as it moves down over the iris. This is the shadow that makes the eye feel set into the face rather than pasted on it, and beginners skip it almost every time.
  • The socket shadow. A very soft, low-contrast darkening around the whole eye — the eye socket is a shallow dish. Keep it wide and faint (10–20% opacity); a tight dark ring around the eye reads as a bruise.

And the light side of the story: a faint reflected light under the lower lid (the cheek bounces light up) keeps the under-eye area from going dead. If you have done the full face shading, these zones line up with the process in our face shading guide — eyes are where that process gets most specific.

Step 6 — Lashes, brows, and the finish

Lashes are the fastest way to over-detail a beginner eye, so treat them as a value, not a drawing:

  • Draw lashes in clumps, not strands. Two to four short strokes per clump, fanning out from the lid edge, darkest at the roots and fading at the tips. The lower lashes are shorter, sparser, and lighter — often just a darkening of the lower lid line.
  • The lash line itself is a darkened band along the upper lid edge, thickest in the middle, tapering at both corners. It is a brush stroke, not an outline.
  • The brow is a shape with a light end, a dark middle, and a tail that thins as it lifts. Paint the mass first, add a few strand breaks with the eraser, and stop.

For hair and brow texture that behaves like real hair, the hair painting guide covers the mass-first approach in full — the same logic applies at brow scale.

Finish with a quick-pinch zoom out and judge the eye at the size it will actually be viewed. If it reads — dark pupil, bright catchlight, shadowed top — it is done. The most common final mistake is adding "just one more detail" at full zoom that only exists at full zoom.

What to do next

Once the process is familiar, speed comes from having brushes that match each step — a clean base brush, a soft blender for the sclera and lid shadows, and fine detail brushes for the iris strokes and lashes. Do the six steps on three different references before you trust them on a finished portrait. Eyes are a repetition skill: the first is slow, the tenth is fast, and the thirtieth is invisible — which is exactly where you want it.
