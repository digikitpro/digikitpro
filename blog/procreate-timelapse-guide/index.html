Procreate Time-Lapse: How to Record and Share Your Art Process

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Every Procreate canvas is secretly a video camera. The app records your whole session automatically — every stroke, every undo, every moment of staring at the canvas deciding what to do — and saves the time-lapse with the file, where it sits until you export it. For most artists, that recording is the most valuable content they will ever make and the one they never touch, because the defaults produce a video that is slow to start, choppy in the middle, and long enough to lose the viewer in the first ten seconds. The recording is free. The watchable version is a settings problem and a session-habit problem, and this guide is both.

It is the companion to the sharing guide — that one covers the finished pieces, this one covers the process — and together they are the complete content workflow: the piece and the making of it, from the same session, with nothing extra to produce.

How the automatic recording actually works

Three facts about the time-lapse that shape everything else:

  • It records everything, including what you remove. The recording is a history of the canvas, not a history of the final piece. A test stroke you erase in five seconds is in the video. An entire failed underpainting you wipe out is in the video — as the thing that got wiped out, which is actually part of the story, but it is a long part of the story if you failed three times. The session habits below are about keeping the history honest without making it a documentary of your doubts.
  • It is a sampled record, not a video. Procreate samples the canvas state over the session and the export plays those samples at your chosen speed. Fast passages compress more than slow ones, which is why the video naturally feels like the work accelerating toward the finish — and why the "choppy" look in very fast sections is the sampling, not a defect.
  • It lives with the file. The time-lapse is part of the .procreate file. Delete the file and the video is gone; keep the file (the backup habit from the fundamentals) and every finished piece carries its own process video forever. This is the part traditional media cannot match: the master file and the process file are the same file.

The session checklist: recording a video you will keep

The time-lapse is made during the session, not after it — the export settings can fix the speed and the start, but they cannot fix the canvas. The checklist, in the order the session runs:

  1. Set the canvas right first. The recording inherits the canvas: a 2000×2500 practice canvas records a smooth, exportable video; a 4K canvas on an older iPad records a slow one. The canvas guide covers the sizes — for a session you intend to share, the medium canvas is the right default, because the video is the deliverable as much as the piece is.
  2. Get the interface out of the way before the first mark. Four-finger tap for full screen, brush and colour panels closed. The time-lapse records the canvas, and the panels are on top of it — a session with the layers panel open for an hour is a video with a layers panel in it for an hour. The habit: full screen before the first stroke, and the panels only when you need them, closed again before you continue.
  3. Keep the setup off the record if you can. The blank-canvas minutes (choosing the brush, setting the colour, the first uncertain strokes) are the part the start-frame setting will cut anyway — so keep them short. The video opens on the first real mark, and the cleaner the first real mark is, the better the opening.
  4. Work in passes, not one continuous agonise. The time-lapse reads as the session's shape: long thinking pauses become long stretches of almost-nothing in the video. Working in passes (a block of strokes, a look, the next block) gives the video rhythm, and the rhythm is what keeps it watchable. This is also just better painting practice — the time-lapse is a mirror, and the mirror is showing you the session you are already having.
  5. Finish the piece before you leave the canvas. The video ends where the session ends, and the ending that works is the finished piece sitting on the canvas for the last few seconds. A time-lapse that ends on a 90% piece is a process video with a cliffhanger nobody asked for — the last 10% (edges, the final pass) is in the recording, so give it the time it takes to be in there.

The export: the settings that matter

Actions → Share → Time-lapse. The export sheet has two settings that do the work:

  • Speed. The playback multiplier. A 3-hour session at 30× exports to a 6-minute video; at 60×, three minutes. For the master export, pick the speed that lands the video in the 5-to-15 minute range — long enough to show the work, short enough to sit on a portfolio page. For the social cut, export the same session at a higher speed (30× to 60×) and trim hard in the editor — the short cut is an edit of the long export, not a separate recording.
  • Start frame. The point in the session where the video begins. Set it past the setup — the first real mark — so the video opens working instead of opening on a blank canvas. If the session had a long failed first approach, the start frame is where that gets cut, and the video opens on the attempt that became the piece.

Two things the sheet does not do, and the editor does: trim the middle (the long pauses, the second and third doubts), and resize to the format (9:16 for vertical, 1:1 for square). Export one MP4 per session at the master speed, and do all the cuts from that one file — the session is recorded once, exported once, and shared as many times as the formats need.

The edit logic that works for the short cut: find the stretch of the session where the work is most legible at speed (for most pieces it is the values block or the colour going on — the moment the piece stops being marks and starts being a picture), cut a 15-to-60 second window around it, and end on the finished piece. Remove the waiting — the thinking pauses are where the cuts go, and the viewer keeps the momentum without ever knowing there was a pause. The finished piece at the end is non-negotiable: the process video's whole promise is "watch this become that," and the "that" is the payoff.

What makes a process video actually get watched

The time-lapse is table stakes — every Procreate artist has one. The ones that get watched are the ones with a shape, and the shape comes from three things the session checklist already produces:

  • An opening that is already working. The start frame set on the first real mark means the video opens in motion. The first three seconds decide, and a blank canvas is three seconds lost.
  • A visible transformation. The cut should contain a before-and-within — the moment the values appear, the moment the colour lands. A process video without a transformation is a screen recording; with one, it is a reveal.
  • A finished ending. The last two or three seconds on the completed piece. This is also the frame the platform shows as the video's thumbnail, and a finished-piece thumbnail outperforms a mid-process one for the same video.

And the caption does the linking: the process post points to the finished piece (the feed post, the pin, the portfolio), and the finished piece's caption points back to the process. The two formats feed each other the way the sharing guide describes — the process converts the viewer into a follower, and the portfolio piece, built on the same sessions, is what the portfolio's process section is made of. One session, four deliverables: the piece, the time-lapse, the social cut, and the portfolio evidence.

What to do next

Next session, run the checklist: medium canvas, full screen before the first mark, passes instead of agonising, the piece finished before the canvas is closed. Then export the time-lapse at 30× with the start frame on the first real mark, and cut one 30-second social version from it. That one session will have produced the piece, the master video, and the post — the complete workflow, and every session after it is the same workflow with the habits already in place.

The pieces the time-lapse is recording are the work of the kits in this library — the pastel washes in this article's images came from the Dreamy Pastel Art Kit, which is the kind of medium (soft, building, visible-in-passes) that reads especially well at time-lapse speed, and every kit in the store works the same way: the brushes are the content, and the recording is free. Start the next session with the checklist, and the process video starts being a by-product of the work instead of a separate project.
