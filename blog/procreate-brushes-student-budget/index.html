How to Build a Procreate Brush Collection on a Student Budget

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

The student brush problem is not that good brushes are expensive — the working kits on this site cost $5, which is less than most art students spend on a single paper. The problem is that the brush market is built for collectors, and the collector's logic (more is better, buy the pack, find the good one later) is exactly the logic that breaks a budget. A student's logic has to be the opposite: the collection is the set of brushes the work actually needs, nothing more, and it grows by one at a time as the work reveals the next gap. This guide is that logic made concrete: what the free tier covers, when the first $5 kit is the right purchase, and the honest math on the bundles that the rest of the market is trying to sell you.

First, the budget frame: what a student brush budget actually is

Three numbers define the frame:

  • The working set is five to ten brushes. A sketch, a liner, a painter, a blender, a texture — the beginner's guide covers exactly this. Everything beyond the working set is for a specific gap the work has revealed, not for the collection's own sake.
  • A focused kit costs $5 and covers a whole category. The kits on this site are built by job (line art, skin, watercolour, texture) rather than by brush count, so one $5 purchase usually closes one gap completely. That is the unit of purchase to think in: gaps, not brushes.
  • The bundle is the only purchase that needs the math. The mega-bundle ($20 for 650+ brushes) is a real value if the kits inside it are the ones you would have bought — and a real waste if they are not. The math is in the section below, and it is the only section of this guide where the answer is "maybe."

The frame's consequence: a full year of serious work, with one kit purchase per term, is under $20 in brushes. That is the budget this guide is built around, and it is enough — the artists who work best on small brush sets are the ones who know their five brushes inside out, which is a depth that no collection size can substitute.

The free tier: what is actually good

The free brush market is 90% filler — packs of 100 where two brushes are decent and the rest are variations of the same idea — and the student budget cannot afford the time cost of sifting it. The free resources that hold up as a complete starting set are the three on this site's freebies page, and they cover the three jobs the working set has:

  • Line and sketch: the Free Fine Liner Set. 100 brushes plus 13 paper textures, covering fine line, sketch and a full paper set — the paper textures alone make it a texture brush as well as a liner. It is the single best free starting point on this site, and the reason it is free is that it is the sample of the rest: the quality standard the paid kits meet is visible in the free pack, which is the honest version of "try before you buy."
  • Value and mark-making: the Chalkboard Artist's Toolkit. 20+ chalk brushes for the value work — the light-and-dark studies, the dry-mark texture, the smudge. Chalk is the best first "painting" medium for a student, because it forgives the flatness that traps beginners in smooth digital paint, and the toolkit is a complete chalk environment, not a single brush.
  • Colour: the Free Color Vault. 1,200+ swatches — not brushes, but the colour reference the mixing work needs, and the skin tone method works directly from it. A colour resource is the free equivalent of a paintbox: it does not replace the brushes, it replaces the guesswork.

And the free instruction: the Procreate Portrait Starter Guide (a free eBook) is the workflow the free brushes are meant to run in — the four-phase process from blank canvas to structured study. The free tier, completely: line, value, colour and workflow, at zero cost. A student who runs the free tier for a month and then still feels a gap has a named gap, which is the only kind of gap worth spending money on.

The first $5: when and what to buy

The first paid kit is the first decision where the budget is real, and the decision rule is simple: buy the kit that covers the gap the work revealed, and nothing else. The gaps, in the order they usually appear:

  1. "My line work feels weak." The gap is a dedicated liner set — the Essential Line Art & Sketch Kit (43 brushes plus a texture bonus) is the focused answer, or the Liner Marker Studio for the inking and marker side of it.
  2. "My portraits are flat." The gap is skin and blending — the Portrait Skin Brushes (19 brushes for pores, texture and the soft blend) closes it, and the skin workflow guide is the technique that uses them.
  3. "My watercolour looks like paint." The gap is the medium's behaviour — the Watercolor Studio Kit (50+ brushes with the bleed and grain the medium needs) is the focused answer, with the watercolour guide as the technique.
  4. "Everything looks the same." The gap is texture — the Premium Texture Effects (a small, focused set) or the Charcoal Studio for the traditional-look work, with the texture techniques guide as the method.

Two purchase rules keep the budget honest. First, one kit per term, maximum — the kit is learned before the next one is bought, and a kit that has not been learned is not a purchase, it is a subscription to a gap that was never closed. Second, the choosing guide before the checkout — it covers the four questions (subject, stroke behaviour, file format, licence) that separate a kit that will be used from a kit that will be collected, and the two minutes it takes are the whole budget strategy for the $5 tier.

The bundle math: the only "maybe" in the budget

The bundles are where the student budget either works or breaks, because they are the only purchase whose value is conditional. The honest math, using the store's own numbers:

  • The individual kits are $5 each (the focused ones) — line art, skin, watercolour, texture, each a complete category.
  • The 650 Mega Bundle is $20 for 650+ brushes and paper textures across every category on the site — the Mega Bundle.
  • The math: four individual kits are $20 — the same as the bundle. So the bundle is a value only if four or more of the kits inside it are ones you would have bought. Three kits you want plus a hundred you will not touch is $20 for $15 of value. Five or six kits you want is $20 for $30 of value, and that is the purchase.

The test that decides it: list the kits you have already identified as gaps (from the $5 section, or from the terms of work so far). If the list has four or more names, the bundle is the purchase — and wait for a promotion if the timing allows, because the bundle's sale price is where the value becomes comfortable. If the list has one to three names, the individual kits are the purchase, and the bundle is a future purchase for when the list grows. The bundle is also the right answer for a specific second situation: the end of a first year, when the collection has proven itself and the question is "do I want the whole range now" — the Master Library ($19, 2,000+ brushes) and the bundle are the two end-of-year answers, and the bundles page compares them side by side.

What the bundle is not: a starter purchase. "I will probably want brushes eventually" is not a gap list, and a bundle bought on a probability is the purchase that fills the student's library with 600 brushes used at five — the exact collector's failure this guide exists to prevent. The organisation system is the other half of the defence: a bundle, if bought, goes into the five-collection structure on import, where the brushes the work reaches for rise to the top and the rest stay where they are.

The plan's property is that it ends with a collection that is fully used, which is the opposite of what the brush market usually produces. Ninety days, under $25, and a library where every brush has a job — that is the student budget working as intended, and it is also, quietly, the professional budget, because the artists with the smallest working sets are usually the ones who know them best.

Where the budget does NOT go

Three places the student brush budget leaks, and the fix for each:

  • The impulse pack. "It is only $3 and it has 200 brushes." The per-brush price is the number the market wants you to see, and the per-gap price is the number that matters: 200 brushes in a pack that does not cover a gap the work has named is 200 brushes you will not use. The fix is the gap list — if the pack does not close a named gap, it does not get bought, at any price.
  • The duplicate. The third liner, the second "professional" sketch set, the pack that does what the pack already in the library does. The monthly sweep is where duplicates die, and the naming convention (Medium – Job) is what makes them visible. A collection with three liners doing the same job is a collection that has stopped growing and started accumulating.
  • The tool gap mistaken for a brush gap. Sometimes the work feels limited and the fix is not a brush — it is the technique (the shading guide, the colour method, the choosing guide), all of them free. The rule: before any brush purchase, one free guide on the suspected gap. Half the time the guide closes the gap and the purchase never happens, which is the best possible outcome for a budget.

What to do next

Start with the free tier this week — the three packs from the freebies page and the free starter guide — and run the first month of the 30-day practice plan on them. The end of day 30 is the first budget decision, and it will be a small one: one named gap, one $5 kit, or no purchase at all if the free tier held. Either way, the decision is made on evidence instead of impulse, and that is the whole game. The beginner's guide names the five brushes the working set needs, the choosing guide runs the four questions before any purchase, and the organisation system keeps the collection the work needs instead of the collection the market sells — the three guides together are the budget strategy, and they are all free, which is the point.
