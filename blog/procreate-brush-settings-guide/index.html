5 Procreate Brush Settings Every Artist Should Customize

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Every brush you buy arrives pre-configured — and the configuration is the artist's guess at how you want to work. Some guesses are right. Most are defaults tuned for a demo, not for your hand. The gap between "this brush is nice" and "this brush is mine" is five settings in Brush Studio, and once you can read those five settings, every brush in your library becomes tunable instead of fixed.

This guide walks through the five settings that actually change how a brush feels — stabilisation, spacing, ink flow, size and edges, and stroke action — with the value ranges that work in practice. It is the companion to the brush buying guide (which covers choosing before you buy) and the brush organisation system (which keeps the customised versions you end up with from turning into a mess).

Before you edit: duplicate the brush

One rule before any of the five: never edit the original. In the Brush Library, tap and hold the brush you want to tune and choose Duplicate. Rename the copy with your naming convention — Ink – Fine (stabilised), Water – Dry Edge — and do all your work on the copy. Two reasons: edits save immediately and permanently to that brush, and a duplicate costs nothing. The artists who get the most from Brush Studio are not the ones with better defaults — they are the ones with a shelf of tuned variants they can fall back on.

To edit, tap and hold the duplicated brush and choose Edit. Brush Studio opens with its tabs along the bottom. Everything in this article is one tab away.

Setting 1 — Stabilisation: the smoothness dial

What it does: Stabilisation (under the Stabilisation tab) smooths your input. A value of 0 means the line goes exactly where your hand goes, wobbles included. A higher value means Procreate averages your recent path and the line follows a smoothed version of it — the stroke starts to feel like it is being guided.

The trade-off: the same setting that cleans up your lines also introduces a slight lag — the stroke follows a hair behind the tip. Too much stabilisation and the brush feels like it is sliding on ice, detached from your hand. Too little and every tremor is in the final line.

Values that work:

  • Linework and lettering: 0.03–0.06. Enough to carry a confident line without the lag becoming obvious.
  • Gestural sketching: 0–0.02, or off. The wobble is part of the information at this stage.
  • Painting brushes: usually leave alone — stabilisation matters less when the mark is a wash than when it is a line, but a small value (0.02–0.04) can calm long edge strokes.

This is the single highest-value setting for beginners, because it attacks the most common complaint ("my lines look shaky") at the source, without changing anything else about the brush. If you only customise one setting per brush, make it this one for your liners.

Setting 2 — Spacing: the texture dial

What it does: Under the Spacing tab, this is the distance between the individual stamps Procreate lays down along a stroke. It is the setting that decides whether a stroke is a continuous ribbon, a textured dash, or a chain of dots — and it is the one that most changes a brush's character per point adjusted.

Values that work:

  • 5–15%: continuous, solid strokes. This is where liners and most painting brushes belong.
  • 20–45%: a visible but connected rhythm — the sweet spot for textured dry-brush effects and most "traditional-look" strokes.
  • 60–100%: clearly separated dashes, dots and chains. Splatter-adjacent behaviour, good for accents, wrong for line work.

The related settings in the same tab (round, linear, angle, jitter) control how the stamps vary — jitter is what makes a dry-brush look random instead of mechanical. A small jitter (5–15%) with medium spacing is the fastest way to make a smooth brush behave like a worn, real one.

Setting 3 — Ink flow and opacity: the build-up dial

What it does: The Loading tab controls how much pigment each stamp deposits (ink), and the Basics tab's opacity controls how strongly the layer shows through. Together they decide whether a brush paints solid on the first pass or builds — and build-up is what makes a digital brush feel like a loaded real one.

Values that work:

  • Flat, solid marks (line art, stamps): ink 100%, opacity 100%. One pass, done.
  • Painting that builds (watercolour, oil, gouache feel): ink 40–70%, opacity 60–100%. Each pass adds a layer of pigment the way real paint does, and you can always go back over an area to deepen it. This is the difference between "coloured in" and "painted."
  • Soft accents: ink 30–50%, opacity 40–60% — the brush becomes a whisper, useful for the first light passes of a wash.

The check: paint a stroke, then paint over it three more times. If the third pass looks identical to the first, the brush has no build and you are one setting away from a different tool. If the fourth pass is dramatically darker than the first, you have a brush that behaves like loaded watercolour — and a reason to slow down, because the build is now part of the technique.

Setting 4 — Size, thin and thick edges: the line-weight dial

What it does: The Basics tab's size is the obvious one — the brush's diameter. The two settings beginners miss are thin edge and thick edge, which shrink the stamp's edge by a percentage. A brush with a 40% thin edge is a smaller, harder brush than the size number suggests; the interaction between size and edge is where the real line weight lives.

What to change, and why:

  • Tighten a fat liner. A liner that is 1.5× your target line width usually wants a 25–40% thin edge rather than a smaller size — the edge control changes how the stroke tapers as well as how wide it is.
  • Soften a hard painter. A painting brush with a hard edge that looks airbrushed-free in the wrong way often wants a small soft edge (10–25%) so its transitions stop looking like cut-outs.
  • Pressure curve. In the Pressure tab, the size-vs-pressure curve decides how much your line weight responds to how hard you push. A gentle curve (small range between min and max) is forgiving for beginners; a steep curve rewards a controlled hand. If your lines look uniformly thin no matter how you press, the curve is flat — drag the middle of the curve down and the brush starts to answer to pressure.

The fastest test: draw a single stroke from light to heavy pressure. You should see a visible gradient of line weight in that one stroke. If the stroke is one width start to finish, the pressure settings are not doing their job, and this is the tab to fix before you blame your hand.

Setting 5 — Stroke action: the behaviour dial

What it does: The Stroke Action tab adds a rule to the stroke itself: the stroke can taper (fade to a point at the end), fade in from the start, vary by velocity, or follow other conditions. It is the setting that changes how a stroke ends — and the end of a stroke is one of the first things the eye checks when judging line work.

The three that earn their keep:

  • Taper out. The stroke ends in a point instead of a dead stop. On liners and sketch brushes this is the difference between drawn lines and cut lines — real pencil lines almost always taper as the hand lifts. A gentle taper (starting around 70–80% of the stroke) is usually enough.
  • Fade in. The reverse — the stroke starts soft and builds. Useful for the first strokes of a shading pass, where you do not want a hard start.
  • Velocity sensitivity. For expressive work, letting the stroke respond to speed (faster = thinner or lighter) adds a calligraphic quality. It is the least predictable of the three — try it on a duplicate, and only keep it if it serves the piece.

Stroke action is the setting most artists never open, which is why default brush strokes all end with the same blunt stop. Adding a taper to your main liner is a thirty-second change that shows up in every line you draw after it.

What to do next

Do the five-settings pass on your three most-used brushes before your next session — duplicate each, tune stabilisation and spacing on the liners, ink flow on the painter, and a taper on the main line. Ten minutes total, and the brushes you reach for every day will stop behaving like the demo artist's brushes and start behaving like yours.

For brushes worth tuning: the Artista Studio Kit is a good set for this exercise because its 76 brushes span the range (liners, sketch, paint, effects), so tuning one of each type teaches you all five settings at once. The Portrait Mastery Kit is the same idea at portrait scale — its blending and texture brushes in particular reward the ink-flow and spacing changes from settings 2 and 3, since portrait work lives on controlled build-up. And when the tuned variants multiply, the five-collection system is what keeps Ink – Fine (stabilised) findable next to Ink – Fine (raw).
