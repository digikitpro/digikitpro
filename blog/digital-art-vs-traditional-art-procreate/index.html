Digital Art vs Traditional Art: What Procreate Beginners Should Know

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

"Should I switch to digital?" is the question traditional artists ask when they are ready to grow, and "Is digital real art?" is the question everyone else asks when a traditional artist says yes. Both questions deserve an honest answer, and the honest answer is the same: neither medium is better — they are different tools with different costs, different failure modes, and different superpowers — and the right choice depends on the work you actually want to make.

This article compares them the way a working artist would: across cost, workflow, texture, mistakes, and the market — with Procreate as the specific digital tool, since that is the app this site exists to help you master. If you are a traditional artist reading this, the goal is a decision you can make in an hour, not a debate you can have for a year.

Cost: two different shapes of spending

The most misunderstood difference, because people compare the wrong numbers. Traditional art is cheap to start and expensive to continue: a pencil and a pad cost almost nothing, but every single piece of work consumes materials — paper, ink, paint, brushes that wear out — and the bill never stops, it just scales with your output. A painter who works daily is buying supplies every month, forever, at a rate that is invisible in any single purchase.

Digital art is the mirror: expensive to start, cheap to continue. The upfront is real — an iPad that runs Procreate comfortably, the app (a one-time purchase), and eventually an Apple Pencil if you want pressure — and it is a few hundred dollars in one decision. After that, each piece of work costs nothing: no paper, no pigment, no wear. The brushes you buy are one-time purchases too, and a focused set covers years of work.

The honest summary: over a year of regular work, digital is almost always cheaper; over a single weekend of trying, traditional is. If you are testing whether you want to make art more seriously, start with what you already own. If you have already decided to work regularly, the digital upfront amortises fast.

Workflow: where digital wins cleanly

Three parts of the workflow are not close, and they matter more than they sound:

  • Undo. This is the superpower, and it is not a metaphor. In traditional media, a bad decision costs a piece of paper or a repainted area; in Procreate, it costs a two-finger tap. The psychological effect is large: artists experiment more, take bigger chances, and finish more work, because the cost of a wrong turn dropped to zero. Everyone who switches notices it within a week, and almost everyone names it as the reason they stayed.
  • Layers and non-destructive edits. Construction, line art, colour, shading and texture each live on their own layer, and each can be reworked without touching the others. A traditional artist achieves the same separation with underpainting discipline and glazes — a real skill, but one that costs mistakes to develop. In Procreate it is the default state of every file.
  • Delivery. A finished Procreate file exports to a print-ready PNG or PDF in seconds, to any size the canvas supports. There is no scanning, no drying time, no shipping a fragile object to a client, no "the colours look different on the print." For commission work and publishing, the delivery gap between the media is the largest practical advantage digital has.

Texture and feel: where traditional still wins

Now the honest other side, because skipping it would make this article a sales pitch.

The physical feedback is real. A pencil dragging across paper grain, the resistance of a loaded brush, the smell of the medium — these are not nostalgia. They are sensory information about the mark you are making, and they shape the work in ways that are hard to quantify and easy to miss until the feedback is gone. Traditional artists who switch report a "too clean" feeling in their early digital work, and the reason is not skill loss — it is that the surface stopped telling them something.

The clean failure mode. Digital art fails in the opposite direction from traditional art: not too rough, but too uniform. Every gradient smooth, every edge even, every surface consistent — and the viewer's eye reads that uniformity as "digital" without being able to name why. This is a solvable problem, and it is the subject of two articles on this site: the seven techniques that make digital look traditional, and the texture techniques guide, which covers paper grain, broken edges, dry-brush marks and the final grain pass in detail. But it is a problem you have to solve — every finished digital piece is a small campaign against uniformity, and traditional media simply does not have that campaign.

The object itself has value. A traditional painting is a physical object — one of a kind, with a surface you can touch, that ages. In the market for original artwork, the object is part of the price. Digital work bridges this with limited, signed, numbered prints, which is a real and growing market — but it is a bridge, and collectors of originals collect originals. If the physical object is part of what you want to make, that is a legitimate reason to keep traditional in your workflow, regardless of everything else in this article.

Mistakes and iteration: the hidden cost difference

Beyond the obvious undo advantage, the two media treat mistakes differently in a way that changes what you make:

  • In traditional media, a mistake is a commitment. Paint dries. Ink soaks. The artist develops strategies around the constraint — underpainting, glazing, working light-to-dark — and those strategies produce a certain calm, planned quality in the work. The constraint is real, and the work it produces is real.
  • In digital media, a mistake is a draft. The same work can be built in any order, reworked at any stage, and the finished piece often carries traces of its iterations — a shadow repositioned three times, a background that changed twice. The work tends to feel more considered, because it was, in a literal sense, considered more times.

Neither approach is superior; they produce different work. The practical note for traditional artists: your planning discipline transfers directly. An artist who can commit to a value structure in gouache will build cleaner digital paintings than an artist who has only worked in undo — the fundamentals are the medium-agnostic part, and they are the part that matters.

Sharing, selling, and the market

The market question is usually asked as "is digital worth less?" — and the honest answer is that the medium has barely mattered to buyers for a decade. What matters is the work, the artist, and the format:

  • Commissions and illustration: clients buy the image. Digital is the industry default for a reason — speed of iteration, easy revisions, instant delivery, print-ready files. A traditional artist competing in this market without a digital workflow is at a structural disadvantage, and it is not about quality.
  • Prints and products: digital is native. A Procreate file at the right canvas size is the print master. Traditional work needs a scan or a photograph to become a print, and the reproduction step loses something the digital file never had to give up.
  • Originals: the one market where the medium still commands a price difference, because the object is the product. Digital artists participate through limited editions — numbered, signed, printed on good paper — which is a real market with real collectors, but a different market from the original-on-canvas one.
  • Social and audience building: the time-lapse is the digital medium's native advantage. Every Procreate session can be exported as a process video — the work showing itself being made — which is content that traditional media can only approximate with a camera on a tripod. For audience growth, process content is one of the most reliable formats that exists, and it is free to anyone who works in Procreate.

What Procreate does for traditional artists specifically

Procreate is the closest digital tool gets to a traditional workflow, and three of its properties matter most for switchers:

  • The brushes imitate media, not just effects. The best digital brush sets are not "textured strokes" — they are simulations of specific behaviours: the crumb and smudge of charcoal, the edge and bloom of watercolour, the drag of a chalk stick. A traditional artist who knows what charcoal does can recognise and drive a good charcoal brush in a way that a digital-native artist often cannot. Your traditional eye is an asset here, not a gap to close.
  • The interface is a studio, not a software. No menus deep three levels down, no file management, no project settings — you open the app and there is a canvas. The closest traditional analogue is a well-stocked table, and the workflow distance between "picks up a pencil" and "starts painting" is what makes the switch feel less like learning software and more like moving studios.
  • The free packs remove the first barrier. You do not need to buy anything to start: the Chalkboard Artist's Toolkit, the Free Fine Liner Set and the Free Color Vault together cover sketching, line work and colour references at zero cost. A traditional artist can spend a full week in Procreate with free brushes and only then decide whether the paid sets are worth it — which is the right order, because the decision should be based on the workflow, not the brush marketing.

The hybrid: what most working artists actually do

The comparison in this article has a winner in every row except one, and the row that does not have a winner is "your work." In practice, the artists who get the most out of both media run a hybrid, and the two most common splits are:

  • Traditional sketch, digital finish. The drawing happens on paper — where the feedback and the feel live — and it is scanned into Procreate for colour, shading and finishing. The line work keeps its paper grain (a scanned pencil line carries real tooth that no brush perfectly replaces), and the painting gets the full digital workflow. This is the most popular split among illustrators, and it uses the strengths of both media in the stages where each one wins.
  • Digital build, traditional output. The piece is made in Procreate and printed on paper — or, for the object market, the digital original is accompanied by a small run of physical prints. The workflow stays fully digital, and the physical object exists at the end instead of throughout.

Whichever split you choose, the fundamentals are the same currency in both: value, composition, colour, drawing. Every hour of practice in either medium is an hour of practice in the art — which is the answer to the question this article started with. The medium is the tool. The work is the point. Pick the tool that gets out of your way most often, and start making.

What to do next

If you are a traditional artist deciding whether to try Procreate, the lowest-risk path is a two-week test: the free packs only, one small piece a day, and a note at the end of each day on what the workflow helped with and what it got in the way of. The 30-day practice plan works well as the structure for that test — its first week is pure line work, which is exactly the stage where a pencil hand feels most at home on a screen.

For brushes built around the traditional behaviours this article described: the Professional Charcoal Studio is the most direct translation of a charcoal workflow into Procreate, the Professional Digital Sketchbook is the complete sketching environment — brushes, paper textures and torn-paper stamps — for artists whose work starts in drawing, and the free Chalkboard Artist's Toolkit is the zero-cost first step that demonstrates the dry-mark behaviour every traditional artist recognises.

And when the clean digital look starts to bother you — it will, around piece three, and that is a good sign — the traditional-look techniques and the texture techniques guide are the two articles that answer it.
