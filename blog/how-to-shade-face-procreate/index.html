How to Shade a Face in Procreate: A Step-by-Step Beginner Guide

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 19 September 2026

Shading is what turns a flat colour map into a face that looks like it occupies three-dimensional space. Without it, a portrait reads as a colouring-book page. With it, the same shapes start to feel like bone, muscle, and skin.

This guide walks through a repeatable shading process you can use on any portrait. It assumes you already have a sketch and a base skin colour in place. If you are still working on the earlier stages, the complete portrait workflow guide covers the full sequence from reference to finish.

What you need before you start shading

Three things should be on your canvas before you begin:

  1. A finished sketch or line art on its own layer.
  2. A flat base skin colour on a separate layer below the sketch. Pick a mid-tone — not the lightest or darkest value of the skin, but a middle value that you can push lighter or darker from.
  3. A reference image with a clear, single light source. Use Procreate's Reference Window (Actions → Canvas → Reference) to keep it visible beside your canvas.

Set your base colour layer as a Clipping Mask target so that all your shading stays within the face shape. Create a new layer above it, set that layer as Clipping Mask, and you are ready to paint shadows without going outside the edges.

Step 1: Decide where your light is coming from

Every shading decision depends on this one choice. Pick a single light source direction and commit to it for the entire painting. The most common beginner-friendly option is a light coming from above and slightly to one side — it creates clear, readable shadows.

Once you have chosen a direction, the face has predictable shadow zones:

  • Under the brow ridge — the eye sockets sit in shadow.
  • Sides of the nose — one side catches light, the other falls into shadow.
  • Under the nose — a small cast shadow on the upper lip area.
  • Beneath the lower lip — a thin shadow where the lip curves back.
  • Under the chin and along the jaw — a cast shadow on the neck.
  • Cheek hollows — the area below the cheekbone, more visible on some face shapes than others.
  • Under the hair or fringe — a cast shadow on the forehead.

You do not need to memorise all of these. Look at your reference and notice where the darks are. Your reference is more accurate than any list.

Step 2: Block in the core shadows

Start with the darkest shadow areas first. On your Clipping Mask layer, use a soft painting brush at around 40–60% opacity and a colour that is darker and slightly cooler than your base skin tone.

A common mistake is reaching for black or dark grey. Skin shadows are rarely neutral grey. They carry colour — usually shifted towards purple, blue, or a deeper version of the base warm tone. If your base skin is a warm peach, try a muted mauve-brown for the shadows. If your base is a deeper brown, try a rich dark umber with a cool bias.

Block in the shadows as flat shapes first. Do not blend yet. Focus on getting the placement and size right. The shadow shapes should follow the form of the face — curved where the surface curves, angular where bone is close to the skin.

Edge quality matters

Not all shadow edges are the same:

  • Hard edges appear where a surface turns sharply (the edge of the nose bridge, the jaw line, the crease under the lower lip).
  • Soft edges appear where a surface curves gradually (the transition from forehead to temple, the roundness of the cheek).
  • Cast shadows (from the nose, chin, or hair) have harder edges near their origin and softer edges further away.

Varying edge quality is one of the fastest ways to make a portrait look less flat. A face shaded entirely with soft edges looks like a blurred photograph. A face shaded entirely with hard edges looks like a comic book. Most realistic portraits sit between the two.

Step 3: Add midtones to connect light and shadow

Between your lightest areas and your darkest shadows, there is a range of midtones that create the gradual transition from light to dark. Without midtones, your shading looks like two flat zones with a hard line between them.

Create another Clipping Mask layer above your shadow layer. Use a colour slightly darker than your base skin tone (but lighter than your core shadow colour) and paint along the edges of the shadow shapes, widening the transition zone. This is where a soft brush at low opacity (20–30%) is useful.

Pay attention to the face's underlying structure. The forehead is a broad, slightly curved plane — its midtone transition is wide and gradual. The nose is a collection of small, sharp planes — its midtone transitions are narrow and quick.

Step 4: Blend selectively

Now you can use Procreate's smudge tool to soften transitions — but selectively. Set the smudge brush to 25–35% opacity and make two or three passes on areas that should look smooth:

  • The forehead
  • The cheek planes
  • The chin

Leave other areas less blended:

  • The crease beside the nose
  • The edge of the jaw
  • The shadow under the lower lip

Over-blending is the most common cause of the plastic, airbrushed look in digital portraits. If everything is smooth, nothing looks like skin. The blending brushes guide covers this in more depth.

Step 5: Add highlights

Highlights are the areas where the light source hits the face most directly. They are usually found on:

  • The centre of the forehead
  • The bridge and tip of the nose
  • The tops of the cheekbones
  • The cupid's bow and centre of the lower lip
  • The point of the chin

Create a new Clipping Mask layer for highlights. Use a colour that is lighter and slightly warmer than your base skin tone. Paint at low opacity (15–25%) and build up gradually. Highlights should be subtle — a common beginner mistake is painting them at full opacity, which makes the face look wet or sweaty.

Keep highlight edges soft on rounded areas (forehead, cheeks) and slightly sharper on bony protrusions (nose bridge, chin).

Step 6: Add reflected light

This is the detail that separates competent shading from convincing shading. Reflected light is the faint illumination that bounces onto the shadow side of the face from the environment — a nearby wall, a shirt collar, a table surface.

On the shadow side of the face, near the edge where shadow meets the background, add a thin band of slightly lighter, slightly warmer colour. This band should be subtle — just enough to suggest that light is bouncing back into the shadow. Without it, shadows look like flat dark patches. With it, the face starts to feel like it exists in a real space.

Keep reflected light weaker than your main highlights. If it becomes too bright, it confuses the light source and the face starts to look like it is lit from two directions.

Step 7: Add skin texture last

Texture goes on after your values (lights, midtones, shadows) are working. Adding texture too early makes it harder to judge whether the shading is correct.

On a new Clipping Mask layer above your shading, use a texture brush at very low opacity (10–20%) to suggest pores, fine lines, and subtle skin variation. Focus texture on areas where it is most visible in real life:

  • The nose and surrounding cheek area (where pores are most prominent)
  • The forehead
  • The chin

Avoid adding texture to areas that should look smooth, like the eyelids or the centre of the lower lip. The skin texture guide explains this process in detail, and the Portrait Skin Brushes kit includes 19 dedicated tools for pores, freckles, wrinkles, and textured blenders designed for this stage of the workflow.

A layer structure for face shading

Here is the layer stack this process produces, from bottom to top:

LayerPurposeType
BackgroundCanvas background colourNormal
Skin BaseFlat mid-tone skin colourNormal
Core ShadowsDarkest shadow shapesClipping Mask → Skin Base
MidtonesTransitions between light and shadowClipping Mask → Skin Base
HighlightsLightest areasClipping Mask → Skin Base
Reflected LightEnvironmental bounce on shadow sideClipping Mask → Skin Base
Skin TexturePores, freckles, fine linesClipping Mask → Skin Base
Line Art / SketchConstruction lines or final inked linesNormal (top)

Each shading layer is a Clipping Mask on the Skin Base, which means you can delete, reorder, or adjust any of them without affecting the others. Our layers explained guide covers how these tools work if you want a deeper understanding.

Common shading mistakes and how to fix them

The face looks flat

You are probably using only two values — light and dark — with nothing in between. Add midtone layers to create a gradual transition. Check that your shadows follow the three-dimensional form of the face rather than sitting on top of it like a pattern.

The face looks dirty

Your shadow colour may be too desaturated or too close to grey. Skin shadows carry colour. Try shifting your shadow hue towards purple, red, or a deeper warm tone. Compare your shadow colour against the reference — if the reference shadows look warm and yours look grey, that is the problem.

The face looks plastic

You are over-blending. Reduce smudge passes, leave some visible brush texture, and add subtle skin texture at the end. The goal is skin that looks like skin, not skin that looks like polished marble.

The highlights look sweaty

They are too bright or too large. Reduce the opacity, shrink the area, and soften the edges. Highlights on skin are usually subtle — a gentle lift in value, not a white spot.

Practise with a sphere first

If the full face feels overwhelming, practise this shading process on a simple sphere. Draw a circle, fill it with a base colour, decide where the light is, and block in shadows, midtones, highlights, and reflected light. A sphere has the same curved-surface shading as a cheek or forehead, but without the complexity of facial features.

Once you can shade a convincing sphere, the same principles transfer directly to the face. The only difference is that the face is a collection of many smaller curved and angular planes instead of one large one.
