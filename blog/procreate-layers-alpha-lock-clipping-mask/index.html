Procreate Layers Explained: Alpha Lock, Clipping Mask & Layer Masks for Beginners

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 19 September 2026

Procreate's layer panel looks simple — a stack of thumbnails with a few options — but three features inside it cause more confusion than anything else in the app: Alpha Lock, Clipping Mask, and Layer Mask.

They all solve variations of the same problem: how to paint, shade, or edit part of your drawing without affecting the rest. The difference is in how they do it, whether the change is permanent, and which workflow they suit best.

This guide explains each tool with plain-language descriptions, practical examples from portrait painting, and a comparison table so you can decide which one to reach for. Everything described here is based on Procreate's official Layers documentation.

What layers do in Procreate

Think of layers as transparent sheets stacked on top of each other. You can draw on each sheet independently. Where one sheet is blank, you see the layers below it. Where it has colour, that colour covers what is underneath.

This separation lets you:

  • Edit one element (like eye colour) without touching another (like the background).
  • Experiment with shading on a separate layer and delete it if it does not work.
  • Adjust opacity, blend modes, and visibility for each layer independently.
  • Reorder elements by dragging layers up or down in the stack.

A typical portrait uses between 10 and 30 layers. A well-organised portrait uses fewer than you think — not more. The complete portrait workflow guide shows a practical layer order from sketch to finish.

Alpha Lock: painting inside existing pixels

What it does

Alpha Lock locks the transparent (empty) pixels on a single layer so you can only paint where colour already exists. Once activated, every brush stroke is constrained to the painted area of that layer.

How to activate it

Swipe right with two fingers on the layer thumbnail. A checkered background appears on the thumbnail to confirm it is active. You can also tap the layer to open its menu and select Alpha Lock.

When to use it

Alpha Lock is best for quick, single-layer edits:

  • Changing the colour of line art. Alpha Lock your line art layer, pick a new colour, and paint over the entire layer. Only the lines change colour; the transparent areas stay empty.
  • Adding texture to a flat shape. Alpha Lock a base colour layer and paint subtle texture on top without going outside the edges.
  • Adding blush or warmth to skin. Alpha Lock the skin base layer and lightly brush a warm tone on the cheeks and nose.

Limitations

Alpha Lock is destructive. Your edits go on the same layer as the original pixels. If you make a mistake, you need to undo or paint over it — you cannot simply hide the layer to see the original. For edits you might want to adjust later, use a Clipping Mask instead.

Clipping Mask: non-destructive edits on a separate layer

What it does

A Clipping Mask is a layer that clips to the visible pixels of the layer directly below it. Anything you paint on the Clipping Mask layer is visible only where the layer beneath has colour. Everywhere else, it is invisible.

How to activate it

Create a new layer above your base layer. Tap the new layer and select Clipping Mask from the menu. A small arrow appears on the thumbnail pointing down to the base layer, confirming the clip.

When to use it

Clipping Masks are the workhorse of portrait painting. Use them whenever you want to add something on top of a shape while keeping it editable:

  • Shadows on skin. Create a Clipping Mask above your skin base layer. Paint shadows using a cool-toned colour at low opacity. If the shadow placement is wrong, delete the Clipping Mask layer and start again — the base layer is untouched.
  • Highlights on hair. Clip a highlight layer to the hair base. Paint bright streaks following the hair direction. Adjust the layer opacity until the highlights look natural.
  • Clothing patterns or textures. Clip a pattern or texture layer to the clothing base colour. The pattern stays within the garment shape automatically.
  • Eye detail. Clip separate layers for iris colour, light reflections, and shadow within the eye shape.

Multiple Clipping Masks

You can stack several Clipping Masks above the same base layer. Each one clips independently to the base. This is how a professional portrait workflow stays organised: shadows on one Clipping Mask, highlights on another, skin texture on a third — all editable, all non-destructive.

The Portrait Mastery Kit organises its 46 brushes into workflow groups (sketch, ink, blend, hair, detail, texture, finish) that map naturally to this kind of layer structure.

Layer Mask: hiding parts of a layer without erasing

What it does

A Layer Mask is a grayscale image attached to your layer. Black areas on the mask hide the layer. White areas reveal it. Grey areas create partial transparency. The original pixels on the layer are never changed.

How to activate it

Tap the layer and select Mask from the menu. A white mask thumbnail appears next to the layer thumbnail. Tap the mask to select it, then paint on the canvas with black to hide areas or white to reveal them.

When to use it

Layer Masks solve a different problem from Alpha Lock and Clipping Masks. They are about removing rather than adding:

  • Removing a background. Instead of erasing the background permanently, paint it out on the mask. If you later want to adjust the edge, paint white on the mask to bring pixels back.
  • Soft-edge transitions. Use a soft brush on the mask to fade the edge of a layer gradually. This is useful for blending reference images, fading textures, or creating atmospheric depth.
  • Compositing. When combining multiple photos or painted elements, Layer Masks let you control which parts of each layer are visible without permanently cutting anything.

The key rule

Black conceals, white reveals. If you paint black on the mask and something disappears, that is working correctly. Paint white to bring it back. The layer's original pixels are always there — the mask just controls visibility.

A practical example: shading a portrait cheek

Here is how all three tools might appear in a single portrait painting session:

  1. Base layer: Paint the flat skin colour on a layer called "Skin Base."
  2. Alpha Lock on Skin Base: Lightly brush a warm blush tone on the cheeks. This is a quick addition and you do not need to adjust it later.
  3. Clipping Mask above Skin Base: Create a new layer, set it as Clipping Mask, and paint cool shadow tones under the cheekbone, jaw, and neck. If the shadow shape is wrong, delete this layer and try again. The base skin layer is unaffected.
  4. Another Clipping Mask above Skin Base: Add a skin texture layer using a pore brush from the Portrait Skin Brushes kit. Keep the opacity low (15–25%) so the texture supports rather than dominates the painting.
  5. Layer Mask on the Background layer: If you want the background to fade softly behind the subject rather than having a hard edge, paint the mask with a soft brush to create the transition.

This sequence — base, Alpha Lock for quick additions, Clipping Masks for editable shading, Layer Mask for edge control — is the foundation of a non-destructive digital painting workflow.

Reference layers: a fourth tool worth knowing

Procreate also has a Reference layer feature that is related but different. When you set a layer as Reference (tap the layer, select Reference), the colour fill tool (dragging a colour from the colour circle) recognises the boundaries on the Reference layer even when you are painting on a different layer.

This is useful when you want to fill colour inside your line art without painting on the same layer as the lines. Set the line art as Reference, create a new layer below it, and drag your colour fills onto that layer. The fills respect the line boundaries.

Common mistakes with layers

  • Applying Alpha Lock and forgetting it is on. If your brush strokes seem to appear only in certain areas, check whether Alpha Lock is active on the current layer. Swipe right with two fingers to toggle it off.
  • Clipping Mask pointing to the wrong layer. A Clipping Mask clips to the layer directly below it. If you reorder layers, the clip may attach to an unexpected base. Check the downward arrow on the thumbnail.
  • Painting on the Layer Mask by accident. If your brush seems to be making parts of your drawing disappear, you might be painting on the mask instead of the layer. Tap the layer thumbnail (not the mask thumbnail) to switch back.
  • Merging layers before you are finished editing. Once you merge a Clipping Mask into its base layer, you lose the ability to edit them separately. Keep layers separate until the painting is truly finished.

Layer limits and canvas size

Procreate sets a maximum number of layers based on your canvas size and your iPad's available memory. A larger canvas means fewer layers. Before starting a complex portrait, check your layer limit by opening the canvas creation panel — it shows the maximum layers for each size.

If you approach the limit, merge layers that you are certain will not change. Start with layers you know are finished (like a background or flat colour base) and keep the active painting layers separate. Our canvas size and DPI guide explains how to balance resolution against layer count.
