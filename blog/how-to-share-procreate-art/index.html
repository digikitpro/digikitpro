How to Share Your Procreate Art on Instagram and Pinterest

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Procreate makes the art; the export and the post are where most of it gets lost. Artists regularly post work that looks worse on the feed than it does on their own iPad — compressed to mush, cropped by an aspect ratio nobody chose deliberately, or exported at a resolution that the platform quietly discards. None of that is the art's fault. It is a settings problem, and it is fixable in one sitting.

This guide is the complete sharing workflow: what to export and at what size for Instagram and Pinterest, how to build a posting rhythm you can actually keep, and how to use process content (the stuff Procreate records for free) to grow the account around the finished work.

First: the canvas decision that everything else depends on

Export quality is capped by canvas resolution — you cannot export detail the canvas never had. If you are setting up for sharing as well as print, the numbers that cover both are in our canvas size and DPI guide. The short version for a social-first workflow:

  • A 2000×2500 pixel canvas (300 DPI) exports cleanly to every social size with room to re-crop, and still prints at A4. It is the default we recommend for work you intend to share.
  • Smaller canvases are fine for quick studies that will live at phone size — but know going in that they are not going anywhere else.
  • The canvas ratio should anticipate the format. A 2:3 canvas (say 2000×3000) posts natively to Pinterest and re-crops to Instagram without losing the artwork; a square canvas is the Instagram default but needs a background to become a pin.

Exporting from Procreate: the settings that matter

Open Actions (the wrench icon) → Share. The formats worth knowing:

  • PNG — the default for posting. Lossless, no compression artefacts, larger files. Use it for everything you post.
  • JPEG — smaller files at the cost of compression. Only useful if you are hitting a file-size limit somewhere; the platforms re-compress anyway, so the saving is marginal.
  • TIFF / PDF — for print orders and archival. Not for social.
  • Procreate file (.procreate) — the full layered file. Keep one of every finished piece; it is your master, and it is what makes "repaint the background" a five-minute job later.
  • Time-lapse (MP4) — the process video, covered in its own guide below.

Two settings inside the share sheet are the ones that bite people: resolution (choose the canvas resolution, not a percentage — the default is usually fine, but check) and background (if your canvas has transparent areas, decide now whether the export gets a white background or stays transparent; a transparent PNG dropped into a post shows up black in some contexts).

Instagram: the sizes that actually display well

Instagram displays at 1080 pixels wide, regardless of what you upload. Uploading 3000-pixel art does not give you 3000-pixel display — it gives Instagram more to compress. The working sizes:

FormatRatioSize (px)Use for
Feed post4:5 portrait1080×1350The main artwork — the most screen real estate in the feed
Feed post1:1 square1080×1080Quick studies, details, flat-lays of work
Reels / Stories9:16 vertical1080×1920Timelapse, process, behind-the-scenes

The practical workflow: export the full-resolution PNG from Procreate, then make the 1080-wide version (any image app can resize; or use Procreate's own canvas at the right size from the start). Post the artwork centred — if the piece is square and you are posting 4:5, give it a clean background (a flat colour pulled from the piece itself is a trick that costs nothing and looks deliberate) rather than letting it crop. Cropping is where finished work goes to die.

Pinterest: the format that works differently

Pinterest rewards the tall format — pins display in a two-column masonry feed, and a 2:3 pin occupies more of the column than a square does. The standard is 1000×1500 pixels (2:3).

Three pin formats that work for art:

  • The artwork pin. The piece on a 2:3 canvas — centred, with enough negative space around it that a title line can sit at the bottom. Titles matter on Pinterest more than anywhere else in this guide: the pin is searchable, so "Procreate portrait — step 4: values" outperforms "untitled 47" every time.
  • The steps pin. A vertical strip of 3–5 process shots (sketch → values → colour → finish) stacked in one 2:3 image. These are the highest-save format in art Pinterest, because they promise a tutorial and deliver a peek at one.
  • The reference pin. A palette, a value study, a brush test — the small images that make the profile look like a working artist's board instead of a gallery of finished pieces.

The one rule: every pin links somewhere. A pin that links to your portfolio, your shop, or the specific article the pin demonstrates is worth five times a pin that links to the Instagram profile — Pinterest is a discovery engine, and it sends people where the link points.

The posting rhythm: consistent, not constant

The accounts that grow on art content are not the ones that post most — they are the ones that post predictably. A rhythm you can sustain for a year beats a sprint you can sustain for a month. The minimum viable rhythm:

  1. One finished piece per week — the Instagram feed post and the Pinterest artwork pin, same image, two platforms.
  2. One process post per week — a timelapse on Reels or a steps pin on Pinterest (or both from the same material).
  3. Two stories or quick posts in between — a detail crop, a work-in-progress, a "what I used" (brushes, palette, canvas size). These are the low-effort posts that keep the account visible on the days you are not finishing anything.

Five posts a week, every one of them made in under ten minutes from work you were already doing. The trap to avoid is the portfolio-or-nothing pattern: three masterpieces in a month, then silence. The algorithm and the audience both reward the steady drip, and the drip is what turns viewers into followers, because followers follow activity, not peaks.

Process content: the free growth engine Procreate gives you

Here is the part most artists do not realise: Procreate records every session automatically. Every finished piece you have is also a video — the complete time-lapse of it being made — sitting in the share menu, waiting. Process content is the highest-engagement format on art accounts (people watch making-processes the way they watch cooking), and you already own the raw material for every piece you have ever finished.

The workflow: export the time-lapse (Actions → Share → Time-lapse), trim it, and cut it to the format — 9:16 for Reels, a shorter cut for a story, the full length for a pin. The settings, the trimming and the format choices are the subject of the time-lapse guide, which also covers the session habits (keeping the canvas clean, the interface hidden) that make the recordings watchable.

Pair the process with the result: the timelapse post ends on the finished piece, and the finished piece's caption points back to the process. The two formats feed each other, and together they answer the only question a new follower has — can this person actually make things? — with a two-minute video instead of an adjective.

Building the account as a portfolio, not a feed

The account is the first portfolio most people will ever see. Three habits keep it reading as a portfolio:

  • A pinned set of best work. The six to nine pieces you would put in a portfolio — pinned to the top of the profile, updated when something better arrives. The rest of the feed is the process; the pinned set is the claim.
  • A consistent visual language. The same export treatment (contrast, sharpness), a small number of formats, and a profile that would recognise its own work out of context. Consistency reads as quality before the first piece is examined.
  • Every finished piece gets its full set. The feed post, the pin, the timelapse, the process notes — the piece is not "shared" until it has been shared everywhere it can work. One piece fully distributed outperforms three pieces half-distributed, because the distribution compounds: the pin finds new people, the timelapse converts them, and the pinned set catches them at the profile.

If the goal is a portfolio that goes further than a feed — for applications, commissions, or a body of work that persists — the portfolio guide walks through structuring the account and a written portfolio around the same pieces, so the social account and the portfolio are one project instead of two.

What to do next

Do this in order, and it takes one evening: (1) check your canvas setup against the canvas guide so future work exports at the right resolution; (2) export your three best recent pieces as full-resolution PNGs and make the 1080-wide Instagram versions and the 2:3 pin versions of each; (3) export the time-lapse from one of them and post the trimmed cut; (4) pin the three best pieces to the top of the profile.

After that, the rhythm from this article runs itself — one finished piece, one process post, two quick posts, every week. And the brushes in the work you are sharing are the story of the work: the pieces in this article's images were painted with kits like the Watercolor Studio Kit and the Dreamy Pastel Art Kit, which is exactly the kind of "what I used" detail that the quick posts in the rhythm are built from — a link to a brush kit in the caption is not advertising when the piece above it was made with it, and it is the most natural product placement there is.
