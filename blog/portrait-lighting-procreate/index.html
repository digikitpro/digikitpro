Portrait Lighting in Procreate: Where to Place Highlights and Shadows

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Lighting is what turns a flat face into a person. Every portrait — painted, drawn, or photographed — is really a record of where a light source hit a three-dimensional head, and the viewer's brain reads that record before it reads anything else. Get the highlights and shadows in the right places and the face pops forward; get them wrong and the most detailed portrait in the world looks pasted on.

This article teaches the five classic portrait lighting setups, exactly where the highlights and shadows fall in each one, and how to map them in Procreate. It is the companion to our face shading guide: that article teaches the shading process, this one teaches the lighting logic behind it.

Find the light before you paint a stroke

Before you place a single shadow, answer three questions from your reference (or decide them yourself if you are working from imagination):

  1. Where is the light? Front, side, above, below? Most portrait light comes from above and to one side. Under-lighting (light from below) reads as horror-film lighting, so unless you want that, avoid it.
  2. How hard is it? A large soft source (a window on an overcast day) gives wide, gentle transitions. A small hard source (a bare lamp) gives tight, dark shadows. The setup name tells you the geometry; the hardness tells you the edge quality.
  3. Where is the brightest spot? On your reference, the brightest patch of skin is your anchor. Everything else — every shadow, every midtone — is measured relative to it.

The fastest way to read a photograph: find the catchlights in the eyes. They are tiny mirrors, and they point straight at the source. Then check that the nose shadow points away from the same spot. If the two agree, you have the light direction.

The five value zones of a lit face

Every lit surface, including a face, has five zones. Learn the names once and every lighting setup becomes a rearrangement of the same five:

  1. Highlight — the brightest zone, where the light hits the surface most directly. On a face: typically the bridge of the nose, the forehead, the tops of the cheekbones.
  2. Light — the broad mid-light zone facing the source: the lit cheek, the lit side of the nose.
  3. Mids — the turn of the form, where the surface begins to face away: the sides of the nose, the edges of the cheeks.
  4. Core shadow — the dark line where the form turns away from the light: under the brow, the shadow-side edge of the nose, the jaw line on the shadow side.
  5. Reflected (bounce) light — light that bounces back off nearby surfaces into the shadow: the chin bounces light under the lower lip and under the nose, the shoulder bounces light into the neck and under-jaw area. Reflected light is never as bright as the direct light — keep it a mid-dark, or the shadow "breaks."

A face lit by a window is just these five zones at a specific arrangement. Change the arrangement, and you change the setup — and the mood.

Setup 1 — Soft (butterfly) lighting

The light sits in front of the face, slightly above — the classic portrait and beauty lighting. Both sides of the face are lit, and the shadows fall down from the features rather than to one side.

  • Highlights: forehead, nose bridge, tops of both cheekbones, cupid's bow, chin.
  • Shadows: under the nose (a small, roughly symmetrical shadow — the "butterfly"), under the lower lip, under the chin (a soft band on the neck), under the brows, and a faint shadow under the lower lid.
  • Feel: flattering, even, calm. The default for headshots and beauty work.

In Procreate: paint the base skin, then the shadow band under the nose and the under-chin band with a soft brush at 20–30% opacity. The symmetrical shadows are the tell — if one side is darker, you have drifted into loop or Rembrandt without meaning to.

Setup 2 — Loop lighting

The light sits 30–45 degrees to the side and above. It is the most common and most forgiving of the directional setups, and the one we recommend beginners learn second (after soft).

  • Highlights: the lit side of the nose, the lit forehead and cheekbone, the lit side of the chin.
  • Shadows: the nose casts a small shadow that loops down and toward the corner of the mouth (that loop is the setup's name), the shadow-side cheek is a broad mid-dark, the brow ridge casts a band across the shadow-side eye socket, and the jaw line on the shadow side carries the core shadow.
  • Feel: natural, dimensional, slightly serious. The workhorse of portrait photography.

The check that tells you you have loop: the shadow-side eye is still visible. The eye socket is shaded, the iris reads, and the nose shadow does not touch the cheek. If the shadow-side eye disappears into darkness, you have slid into split lighting.

Setup 3 — Rembrandt lighting

The light sits further to the side (about 45 degrees) and higher than loop. It is the most recognised dramatic portrait lighting, and the one with the most specific signature:

  • Highlights: the lit side of the face as in loop, plus the forehead and the top of the nose.
  • Shadows: the shadow side of the face is mostly dark — except for one deliberate triangle of light on the shadow-side cheek, directly under the eye on that side. The triangle is formed by the brow shadow and the cheekbone shadow meeting at an angle. The nose shadow extends across the bridge toward the shadow side.
  • Feel: dramatic, classical, intimate. The look of a thousand old-master portraits for a reason.

The triangle is the whole setup. It should be roughly the size of the eye, about twice as long as it is wide, with its point aiming toward the corner of the mouth. If the triangle is too big, the light is too high; if it is too small or gone, it is too low. In Procreate, paint the shadow side first (a broad soft darkening), then open the triangle with a lighter tone — that order keeps the triangle crisp instead of smudged.

Setup 4 — Split lighting

The light sits directly to the side, at eye level or slightly above. The face divides into a lit half and a shadow half, split roughly down the nose.

  • Highlights: the lit half of the nose, the lit cheekbone, the lit side of the forehead.
  • Shadows: the entire shadow half — cheek, jaw, eye socket, and half the nose — falls into a single broad shadow. The catchlight on the shadow-side eye may be all that is visible of it.
  • Feel: stark, graphic, noir. Strongest of the five; use it when the subject can carry the drama.

Split is the setup beginners over-use because it is the easiest to fake (paint one side darker) and the easiest to over-do (the shadow side goes black). Two rules keep it working: the shadow side is dark mid-tone, not black — you should still read the cheekbone and the jaw in it — and the shadow edge follows the nose's real turn, not a straight line down the middle of the face.

Setup 5 — Rim (back) lighting

The light sits behind the subject, and the face itself is in shadow, lit only by ambient or bounce. The light appears as a bright edge — the rim — around the head, hair and shoulders.

  • Highlights: a bright line along the top of the head, the hairline, the shoulders, and the edge of the jaw that faces the light. The face's own features get only faint fill.
  • Shadows: the entire front of the face is the shadow zone; features read as dark shapes with subtle reflected light — under the nose, under the lower lip, the chin catching the last of the bounce.
  • Feel: mysterious, cinematic, high-contrast. The setup for silhouettes that are not quite silhouettes.

In Procreate: build the rim as its own layer — a bright, slightly soft edge around the silhouette — then paint the face as a near-shadow study underneath. The eye catchlights are the one bright thing allowed inside the shadow, and they sell the whole effect.

Mapping light onto facial structure

Setups describe the geometry. Anatomy decides the details. Here is where the light actually lands on a standard three-quarter face, lit from the front-left:

  • Brow ridge: the top of the brow catches light; the underside casts the eye-socket shadow. The socket shadow is one of the largest value jumps on the face — get it soft and wide, or the eyes look sunken.
  • Nose: the bridge is a highlight running between the eyes; the lit side is light; the shadow side is the core shadow; the tip is a small highlight; under the nostril is a small but dark shadow.
  • Cheeks: the top of the cheekbone is the highlight, the hollow under it is a soft shadow that defines the face's width. In frontal setups both sides carry this; in side setups only the lit side does.
  • Lips: the top edge of the upper lip is a shadow line, the cupid's bow is a small highlight, the body of the lower lip is the lip's main highlight, and the under-lip area is a shadow with a bounce light at its bottom edge. The lips guide builds these exact zones step by step.
  • Chin and neck: the chin is a highlight in frontal lighting; the under-chin is the face's biggest shadow; the neck receives the bounce that keeps the jaw from floating.

If you are building a full portrait, the portrait workflow guide sequences these zones into the overall painting order, and the realistic skin guide shows how the values sit on top of the skin's colour — lighting and colour are separate decisions that have to agree.

Reflected light: the zone beginners delete

The single most common lighting error in digital portraits is painting the shadow side of the face as dead black. Real light does not stop at the core shadow — it bounces. The chin throws light back under the lower lip and under the nose; the shoulder throws it back into the neck; the environment throws a faint ambient across the whole shadow side.

The rule: the darkest value in your portrait should be the eye sockets and the pupil, not the shadow cheek. Paint the shadow side as a dark mid-tone, then add the bounce lights (under the lip, under the nose, under the chin) as slightly lighter patches inside it. The shadow then reads as a place with depth in it, instead of a hole.

Common lighting mistakes (and the one-minute fix for each)

  • Highlights in the wrong place. The brightest spot should be where the light actually hits — if your highlight is on the shadow-side cheek, the light source has moved. Fix: find the brightest spot, and move every other highlight to agree with it.
  • Hard shadow edges on a soft light. A soft source gives soft edges. If your shadows have crisp lines under a "window" light, the edge quality disagrees with the source. Fix: run a low-opacity blender over the core shadows until they match the source's softness.
  • No reflected light. Shadow side goes black. Fix: one bounce light under the lower lip, one under the nose, and a faint ambient across the shadow cheek — 10–20% opacity each.
  • Flat midtones. The face is "fine" but lifeless — usually the midtones between light and shadow are missing, so the face jumps from bright to dark with nothing in between. Fix: build the mids zone deliberately on the turn of the nose, cheek and jaw before you touch the core shadows.
  • Catchlights that disagree with the shadows. The fastest way to make a face feel wrong. Fix: one catchlight position, both eyes, aligned with the highlight map.
