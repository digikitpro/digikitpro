How to Organize Your Procreate Brushes: A Simple System That Works

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Every brush library starts out tidy. Then a free pack here, a bundled set there, a "just one more" download, and the library becomes a wall of thumbnails where finding the one good watercolour blender takes longer than painting with the one you already know. The problem is never the number of brushes. It is the search cost — the seconds you spend scrolling, and the micro-decisions of which similar brush to use, that add up over a drawing session.

This article is a complete organising system for Procreate's Brush Library: how collections actually work, a five-collection structure that scales to hundreds of brushes, a naming convention that finds things for you, and a ten-minute ritual that keeps it that way.

Why a messy library slows you down

Three things happen in an unorganised library, and none of them feel like much in the moment:

  • Decision fatigue. When six similar pencil brushes sit side by side, you spend ten seconds choosing and two minutes doubifying the choice. Artists with tight, known sets paint faster because the brush is never in question.
  • Silent duplication. You downloaded a liner pack, then a "professional liner" pack, then a free one. Three of them do the same job. You keep all three because deleting feels wasteful, and the library gets heavier.
  • Lost favourites. The one textured blender you love is buried under the fortieth thumbnail. You stop using it, forget it exists, and eventually buy a kit that contains a similar brush you do not know about.

None of this is a character flaw. Procreate's default import behaviour puts new sets wherever they land, so every library drifts without a system. The fix is structure, not discipline.

How Procreate collections actually work

Before the system, the mechanics. In the Brush Library (the icon with the stacked brushes in the top toolbar), your brushes live in collections — labelled folders. Collections can contain other collections, so you have one level of nesting to work with.

The useful behaviours:

  • Drag to move. Drag any brush onto a collection (or into one) to move it. Drag a collection onto another to nest it.
  • Import straight into a collection. When you import a .brushset file, you can choose which collection it lands in, instead of letting it pile up in the default spot.
  • Reorder freely. Drag collections to reorder them. Keep the order the way you think, not alphabetical — your workflow order beats dictionary order.
  • Delete only when empty-safe. Deleting a collection deletes its brushes. Move them out first if you want to keep them.

One thing Procreate does not do: sync brushes between iPads. A pack imported on your studio iPad is not on your kitchen iPad. Keep the .brushset files in iCloud Drive (or wherever you store your downloads) and re-import on the device that needs them — our brush installation guide walks through the full import flow, including where files land and the fixes when they do not.

The five-collection system

Create five top-level collections and keep them in the order you use them in a painting. Every brush you own belongs in exactly one of these. If you cannot place a brush, that is information — it usually means it is a duplicate or an experiment, and it belongs in a sixth, clearly-labelled Sandbox collection.

  1. 01 Sketching — construction pencils, sketch pencils, chalk, anything you use before the drawing is committed. This is where the rough work lives.
  2. 02 Linework — liners, inks, markers, technical pens. The brushes that define edges. Keep your three or four daily drivers at the top of this collection.
  3. 03 Painting & Blending — watercolours, oils, acrylics, smudges, blenders. The brushes you hold for the longest part of a session.
  4. 04 Texture & Effects — paper grain, splatter, charcoal smudge, glitter, smoke, grain overlays. The finishing-pass brushes, including dedicated texture packs.
  5. 05 Hair & Detail — hair strands, freckles, pores, fine detail. The small-brush collection, where size matters more than behaviour.

Two conventions make the system work. First, the numeric prefixes: they force an order, and the order you want is workflow order — you sketch before you ink, you ink before you paint, you paint before you texture. Second, the Sandbox habit: anything new goes in Sandbox until you have used it three times. After three uses, it graduates into the right collection or gets deleted. That one rule stops 90% of the clutter before it arrives.

A naming convention that scales

Collections get you to the right shelf. Naming gets you the right brush. Procreate brush names come from the pack author, and they tend to be marketing copy ("Pro Ultra Soft Dreamy Blend!!") rather than a description of what the brush actually does. Rename the ones you use. The pattern that holds up:

[Medium] – [Job], for example Chalk – Sketch, Ink – Fine Liner, Water – Bleed, Oil – Blend.

The rules:

  • Two words, dash between them. The medium tells you what it feels like, the job tells you what it is for. Both are searchable, both are short enough to read at thumbnail size.
  • Never use numbers or "copy" in names. "Pencil 12 (2)" is how favourites get lost. If you need a variant, name it by behaviour: Pencil – Soft, Pencil – Dry.
  • Keep pack names in your notes, not in the brush. If you want to remember where a brush came from, that is metadata for your own notes file, not for the brush library.

Taming the import pile

The messiest moment in any brush library is the moment of import. Three habits keep it clean at the source:

  1. Import one set at a time, and place it. Do not queue up five .brushset files and import them in a batch, then sort the wreckage. One import, choose the destination collection, done.
  2. New sets go to Sandbox, not to your working collections. You have not decided yet whether they are good. Let three uses make the decision.
  3. Delete the file from your download location only after import succeeds. The .brushset file in iCloud or Files is your backup. Keep your downloaded packs in one folder so a new iPad setup is a ten-minute re-import job.

If you are unsure whether a new pack is worth importing at all, the brush buying guide covers how to evaluate a pack by subject, stroke behaviour and workflow fit before you commit.

The favourites workflow

Organising is about finding brushes when you do not know which one you want. Favourites are about the brushes you do know you want. In the Brush Library, the brushes you use most appear at the top of the recent list automatically — but you can tighten this by deliberately keeping only ten to fifteen brushes in active rotation.

The practice: at the start of each session, glance at your five collections and pick the four to six brushes the piece will actually use. They rise to the top by usage. At the end of the session, if a brush did not earn its place, let it drop out. Your working set should feel like a tool belt, not a warehouse.

This is also where the stamp versus painting brush distinction pays off: stamp brushes (stamps, splatters, ready-made elements) belong at the end of your session, and keeping them in the 04/05 collections keeps them out of the way while you paint.

A ten-minute monthly maintenance ritual

The system holds itself together only if something touches it once a month. Set a recurring note — first Sunday of the month, ten minutes:

  1. Sweep the Sandbox. Each brush gets one question: used it three times? Yes → move it to its collection. No → delete it.
  2. Find the duplicates. Scan 01 through 05 for brushes that do the same job. Keep the one you reach for. Delete the rest — or move them to a single Archive collection if you cannot bear it, and never open Archive again this year.
  3. Rename anything generic. One rename per brush, using the Medium – Job pattern.
  4. Reorder the collections. If your workflow has changed (you ink less, you texture more), move the collections so the order matches the new habit.

Ten minutes. The library you are left with is smaller than the one you started with, and faster to use — which is the entire point.
