Your First 30 Days in Procreate: A Daily Practice Plan for Beginners

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

The most common way beginners learn Procreate is by opening the app and drawing whatever is in their head, a little every few days. It feels productive, and it is not — random sketching reinforces whatever you are already bad at, because you never isolate a skill, practise it deliberately, and then test it again. Structured practice does the opposite: one skill per day, a repeatable exercise, and a check that you can see the difference.

This is a complete 30-day plan. Each day has a single focus and a 25-to-40-minute exercise. Week one builds line control, week two builds values, week three builds colour, and week four puts all three into finished pieces. You will need nothing but Procreate — the free packs mentioned at the end are optional, and the plan works with whatever brushes you already have.

How to use this plan

Five rules, and the plan works only if you keep them:

  1. Same time every day. Practice is a habit before it is a skill. Anchor it to something you already do — after breakfast, before work, with the evening tea.
  2. One canvas size for all 30 days. Pick a medium canvas (a 2000×2500 pixel setup at 300 DPI is a good practice default — our canvas size and DPI guide explains the numbers) and keep it. Changing canvas size changes how brushes feel, and you do not want that variable in a skill-building month.
  3. Keep every piece. Name files by date (Day 01, Day 02…) so the Gallery becomes a timeline. You will not feel the improvement on day 15. You will see it on day 30, comparing against day 1.
  4. Do not judge, only note. One line per day in a note: what felt hard, what got easier. Two sentences, no more.
  5. Rest days are in the plan. Days 7, 14, 21 and 28 are review days. Review is part of the practice.

Week 1 (Days 1–7): Line control and shapes

The goal of the first week is a confident line — one that starts where you intend, goes where you intend, and stops where you intend. Every drawing you will ever make starts with this, and it is trainable in days, not months.

Day 1 — Freehand lines. Fill one canvas with horizontal lines, left to right, varying the length. The drill: start each line before you think. Wobbles are data, not failure. Then fill a second canvas with vertical lines, top to bottom.

Day 2 — Circles and ovals. Draw 30 circles of varying sizes. Then 30 ovals at different angles. Notice that circles fail at the bottom — that is normal, and it tells you where to slow down next time.

Day 3 — Basic shapes. Squares, triangles, and rectangles, as many as you can fit on a canvas. For the squares, try the draw-and-hold trick: finish the shape and keep holding, and Procreate's QuickShape snaps it straight. Feel the difference between your line and the snapped one.

Day 4 — Contours. Draw a single continuous line that follows the outline of an object in front of you (a mug, a plant, your hand) without lifting the pencil and without looking at the canvas — only at the object. This builds the hand-eye connection that every other skill depends on.

Day 5 — Hatching and shading marks. Cover small squares with parallel lines in three directions: vertical, horizontal, 45 degrees. Then cross-hatch a few. This is the vocabulary of shading before you ever use a paint brush.

Day 6 — Three objects, line only. Pick three simple objects from your desk. Draw each one on its own layer (yes, layers — start the habit), line work only, no shading, no colour. Twenty minutes total. The constraint is the exercise.

Day 7 — Review. No new drawing. Open Day 1 and Day 6 side by side. Write one line about what changed. Export the whole week's files to your backup location.

Week 2 (Days 8–14): Value and light

Value — the lightness or darkness of a colour — is the skeleton of drawing. Artists who understand value can make a flat, grey-scale image read as three-dimensional, and everything in colour builds on top of it.

Day 8 — The five-step value scale. Make a row of five boxes: pure white, three grays, pure black. Now make the same row again, but start from 20% gray and end at 80% gray — a limited range. The second row is the one that makes drawings look sophisticated. Limited range is control.

Day 9 — Two-tone drawing. Draw one of yesterday's objects using only two values: light and dark. No midtones allowed. This forces you to decide what is in the light and what is in shadow, which is the whole game.

Day 10 — The value sphere. Draw a sphere with five zones: highlight, light, midtone, core shadow, and the reflected light at the bottom edge. Get the core shadow to wrap the form — it is the band that makes a circle into a ball.

Day 11 — Light from the left, light from the right. Draw the same simple object twice, once lit from each side. Keep everything identical except the shadow direction. This trains you to place light deliberately instead of accidentally.

Day 12 — The value cube. A cube from a three-quarter view, fully shaded: three visible faces at three different values, a core shadow at the edges, a reflected light on the darkest face. If the sphere made sense, the cube should too. If it does not, redrew the sphere.

Day 13 — Still life in values. Three objects together on a surface, full value study: cast shadows on the surface, contact shadows where each object touches it, reflected light bouncing between them. This is the first composition exercise — objects arranged, light consistent across all of them.

Day 14 — Review. Compare Day 8 and Day 13. The value scale should look less muddy, the shadows more deliberate. Export the week.

Week 3 (Days 15–21): Colour fundamentals

Colour is where most beginners add mess instead of meaning, because they pick colours by name ("the lips should be red") instead of by value and temperature. This week replaces name-based colour with system-based colour.

Day 15 — The three-swatch scene. Choose exactly three colours from the palette and paint a simple scene (your still life, a window, a landscape photo you like) using only those three. The constraint is the lesson: limited palettes make every colour choice visible.

Day 16 — Warm and cool. Paint the same scene twice: once pushing every colour warmer (reds, oranges, yellows), once cooler (blues, greens, violets). Notice how the temperature alone changes the mood — no other variable moves.

Day 17 — Monochrome. One hue, five values. Paint a simple portrait or object study entirely in shades of a single colour (a muted blue-grey is a good choice). Monochrome work is the fastest way to see whether your values are carrying the drawing — and they must, because there is nothing else to hide behind.

Day 18 — The five-colour palette. Build a palette of five related colours — one light, one mid, one dark, plus two accents — and paint a piece with it. Save the palette. You will rebuild versions of it for weeks, and that is correct: professionals work from a small set of trusted mixes.

Day 19 — Value first, colour second. Do a full value study (grey only) of a face or an object. Then, on a layer above, add colour with a blend mode set to multiply or simply a low-opacity base — the values do the work, the colour just names them. This order, values before colour, is the single most reliable way to avoid muddy, flat colour.

Day 20 — Four-colour portrait study. A face from a front or three-quarter reference, built from your five-colour palette reduced to four: a base, a warm shadow, a cool shadow, and a highlight. No detail work — planes and values only. If the face reads at thumbnail size, you have succeeded.

Day 21 — Review. Compare Day 15 and Day 20. The colour should sit on the values instead of replacing them. Export the week.

Week 4 (Days 22–30): Putting it all together

The final week applies the month's skills to finished pieces, including the two features that trip up portrait beginners most — eyes and lips — plus a first texture pass.

Day 22 — Portrait construction. Build a head from shapes: the cranium, the jaw, the eye line at mid-height, the nose at the quarter point below the eyes, the mouth at the remaining third. No rendering — structure only, on a sketch layer.

Day 23 — Portrait values. The same construction, now fully shaded in values: the eye sockets, the nose bridge, the under-chin shadow, the cheek planes. Reference helps; a plain front-on photo of a friend is enough.

Day 24 — Eyes. Paint both eyes of the constructed portrait: shape, base, iris, pupil, catchlight, and the shadow the upper lid casts. Our step-by-step eye painting guide is written for exactly this stage — follow it in parallel with the exercise.

Day 25 — Lips and the final features. The same portrait, now with lips, brows, and a light pass of hair mass (not strands — mass). The lips guide covers the shape, shadow and shine in five steps.

Day 26 — Texture practice. A new canvas: paint a simple surface (a wall, a wooden table, skin) flat, then add texture on top using a rough brush at low opacity. The goal is to feel where texture helps and where it becomes noise. The texture techniques guide gives you seven specific ways to do this.

Day 27 — Backgrounds and edges. Take your day-25 portrait and add a simple background, then spend twenty minutes only on edges: which edges of the figure should be crisp, which soft. Edge control is what separates a "study" from a "piece."

Day 28 — Review and rest. Compare the week. Export everything. Take the evening off — seriously, close the app.

Day 29 — A piece from reference. A complete, finished piece from a photo reference: construction, values, colour, features, texture, background. Full process, one session, no undo regrets.

Day 30 — A piece from imagination. The same process, but no photo — a subject you choose, a composition you decide. Then the final step of the whole plan: open Day 1 and Day 30 side by side. Write one paragraph about what you can do now that you could not do a month ago. That paragraph is the point of the 30 days.

Tracking your progress (and why it matters more than talent)

The dated file stack is the tool that makes this work. On day 15, comparing yesterday's piece to today's will tell you almost nothing — the signal is too small. On day 30, comparing against day 1 tells you everything, because 30 deliberate hours is a large enough sample to see the trend.

Three habits keep the stack honest:

  • Never delete a day's piece. The pieces you are least proud of are the most important evidence. Improvement is the distance between them.
  • Export weekly, not daily. One backup of the week's files (Procreate files, which keep your layers, or PNGs if you just want the images) to iCloud Drive or a folder you trust. Thirty days of work should never live in one place.
  • Write the one-line note. "Hand trembles less on horizontal lines." "The core shadow finally wraps." The notes are a second timeline, and they tell you which skill to spend the next month on.
