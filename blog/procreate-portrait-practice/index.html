How to Practice Portrait Drawing in Procreate: A Study Method for Beginners

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Portrait beginners usually practice the wrong way: they sit down with a reference, draw a face, feel vaguely unsatisfied, and start the next one. Nothing is isolated, nothing is checked, and the dissatisfaction repeats on face number forty because the method never changes. Practice only improves the thing it measures — and if the session has no measurement in it, the session produces repetition, not progress.

This article is a complete portrait study method for Procreate: how to choose the three kinds of reference (and when each one is the right tool), a five-step weekly cycle that builds a face from structure to finish, and the self-critique checks that tell you where the drawing actually disagrees with the reference. It assumes you can hold a brush and understand layers — the portrait workflow guide covers the full painting process, and this guide is the practice structure around it.

The three kinds of reference, and what each one teaches

Beginners use one reference type (usually a photo of a person) for everything, and hit a wall when it stops being enough. There are actually three tools, and each one teaches a different layer of the skill:

  • Structure references. Cranium-and-jaw diagrams, the Loomis or similar head-construction breakdowns, mannequin heads. These teach the geometry — where the features sit, how the skull turns, what the planes are. Use them in week one, and keep using them as warm-ups for the first few months. A face drawn from construction knowledge is a face you can place in any pose; a face copied from a photo is a face you can only reproduce at that exact angle.
  • Value and feature references. Photos with good, directional light — this is the workhorse of the study practice. The light does the teaching: it shows you the eye socket as a shadow volume, the nose as a light band, the lips as a small form with its own light and dark. Front-lit or flat-lit photos teach less, because there is less to see; choose references the way you would choose a lighting setup, and the portrait lighting guide names the five setups to look for in a photo.
  • Feature references. Close-ups: eyes from the eye study, lips from the lips guide, a nose, a jaw. These are the 15-to-30-minute drills that build the small skills the full face depends on, and their brevity is the point — you can do five mouth studies in the time one full face takes, and the mouths come out of it ten times better.

The progression across a term of practice is: structure references while learning the geometry, value references while learning to see, feature references for the weak spots the full faces keep exposing. The references are not interchangeable — a construction diagram will not teach you what a catchlight looks like, and a photo of a beautiful face will not teach you where the jaw actually is when the head turns.

The five-step weekly cycle

Once a week, one full face, built in five deliberate steps. The canvas is a 2000×2500 pixel practice setup (the canvas guide covers the numbers), and the target time is 60–90 minutes. Each step has a job, and the step is not done until the job is done — moving on early is where the practice quietly stops being practice.

  1. Structure (15 min). From a three-quarter reference: cranium, jaw, eye line, nose line, mouth line, on one sketch layer at low opacity. The check before moving on: the thumbnail test at 100 pixels — does it read as a head at that angle? If not, the structure is wrong and the rest of the face will not save it.
  2. Values (20 min). Grey only, no colour: the five zones (highlight, light, mid, core shadow, bounce — the face shading guide maps them on a real face) painted as planes. The squint check: blur both the reference and the drawing and compare the dark/light pattern. This is the step beginners rush and the step that decides 80% of whether the face works.
  3. Features (25 min). Eyes, nose, lips, brows — each on its own layer, each started from the reference at full zoom and checked at working zoom. This is where the feature references from the drills pay off: the eyes and lips that took you twenty minutes last month take ten now, and the time goes into the nose and the transitions instead.
  4. Colour (20 min). The five-swatch palette from the skin tone mixing guide, applied over the values — values first, colour second, every time. The check: does the colour sit on the structure, or does it flatten it? If the planes disappeared when the colour went on, the colour is too even.
  5. Finish and critique (20 min). Edges, a light texture pass, and then the part that matters most: the difference method, described next. The drawing is not finished at step five — it is finished at the critique, because the critique is where the next session's target gets chosen.

Four weeks of the cycle is roughly two hours of focused face time a week — a load that survives a term of other obligations, which matters more than the intensity. The 30-day practice plan uses the same daily/weekly logic for the fundamentals before you reach the portrait work; if you have not done its weeks one and two, the structure step of the cycle will feel harder than it needs to.

The self-critique: where the actual learning happens

Three checks, in order of what they catch. Each takes under a minute, and each one should end in a written note — one line, in the file name or a notes file: jaw too narrow; eye socket shadow too small; colour flattened the planes. The notes are the curriculum for next week.

  • The thumbnail test (structure). Shrink the face to about 100 pixels. At that size, detail cannot hide a wrong structure — if the head does not read as the right shape at the right angle, no amount of feature work fixes it, and the note goes to structure. This is the check that catches the "it looks close up but wrong from an arm's length" failure, which is the failure that makes a portfolio piece look amateur at the size a viewer actually sees it.
  • The squint test (values). Squint until the drawing blurs into soft shapes. Do the same to the reference. The two blur patterns should match — same darks, same lights, same places. Where they disagree is where the value drawing is wrong, and value errors are the errors that read as "off" before anything else. The most common finding: the shadows are too light (the face is flat) or the shadow side is missing its bounce (the face is muddy).
  • The difference method (details). Put the reference and the drawing side by side, same size, and look only at the places they disagree — not the drawing, not the reference, the gap. Read off three gaps, biggest first. Those three gaps are the entire practice list for the next session, and they are personal: nobody else's tutorial will name them, because they are yours.

One discipline note: the critique has to be written, not just thought. A thought-about failure evaporates in the next session; a written one is a task with an owner. The artists who improve fastest on a study schedule are not the ones who draw the most — they are the ones whose notes file shows the same three problems getting smaller, week over week.

Feature drills: the fast feedback loop

Between the weekly faces, the feature drills run on their own clock — 15 to 30 minutes, any day, in any quantity. The loop that makes them work:

  1. Pick the feature the last face exposed. The critique notes decide — if the eyes were the gap, the eyes get the drill. Not the feature you find pretty, the feature that is costing you.
  2. Do three to five of them, small. Five eyes at 400×400 pixels, or five mouths. The small size forces the decision-making (what is essential at this size?) that the full face hides.
  3. Rank them at the end. Which one worked best, and why — in one sentence. The ranking is the real data; the drawings are just the samples.

The drills and the weekly face feed each other: the face finds the weak spot, the drill fixes it, the next face confirms it. Two or three weeks of this cycle on a single feature (eyes, for most beginners) will do more for the portrait than a month of full faces, because the feature is now a solved problem the face can rely on.

Tracking the term: what "better" actually looks like

Portraits improve in a staircase, not a line — several weeks of no visible change, then a step where the faces suddenly hold together. The tracking that keeps you on the staircase through the flat parts:

  • A dated study stack. Every weekly face named by date, kept forever. The day-30 face next to the day-1 face is the only evidence that matters, and it is evidence no amount of daily discouragement can argue with.
  • The notes file as a progress bar. The same gap named three weeks in a row, then gone, is a completed task. Reading the file backwards at the end of a term is the most motivating document an artist can have.
  • One finished portrait a month. Once a month, the cycle runs its full length with no time limit — a real portrait, not a study, the full workflow applied end to end. The studies are the practice; the monthly portrait is the test, and it is also the piece the portfolio is eventually built from.

When to move from practice to instruction

The method above is self-contained, and it will take a beginner from "faces are hard" to "faces are a process" in a term of consistent work. The point at which structured instruction starts paying back is when the critique notes stop producing new problems — when the same three gaps keep coming back and the difference method is telling you things you cannot act on. That is the signal to bring in a teacher's eye, and the two resources that fit it on this site are the free Procreate Portrait Starter Guide (a four-phase workflow you can follow piece by piece while doing the cycle) and the Complete Procreate Portrait Masterclass — the full sequence from reference to final polish, which is effectively this method at length, with the decision points spelled out. The Portrait Mastery Kit is the toolset side of the same stage: its collections are organised by the workflow steps in the cycle, so the practice and the brush library stop being two separate systems.

What to do next

Start smaller than feels useful: this week, do one feature drill (five of the feature your last face got wrong) and one five-step face with the three critique checks written down. That is the whole method — everything else is repetition of it. In four weeks, the study stack will have four dates in it, and the notes file will have a curriculum. That is how portrait practice compounds: slowly, measurably, and only where the notes say it should.
