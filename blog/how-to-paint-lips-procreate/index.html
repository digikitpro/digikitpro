How to Paint Lips in Procreate: Shape, Shadow and Shine

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Lips are the smallest feature on the face and one of the fastest to look wrong. That combination — small, high-contrast, front and centre — means the eye checks them constantly, and a mouth that is 5% off in shape or value makes a whole face feel unfinished. The fix is not more detail. It is a short, ordered process: shape, pigment, depth, volume, and a shine that earns its place. Five steps, each one a value decision, and each one checkable at the size the lips will actually be viewed.

This guide assumes your face is at the values stage or beyond — the skin is painted, the major shadows are in place. If you are still building the overall shading, the face shading guide covers that first, and the portrait workflow guide shows where the lips sit in the full sequence (they come late, on purpose).

Know the shape in sixty seconds

Before any colour, the mouth is four structural facts:

  • The cupid's bow — the double curve at the top centre of the upper lip. It is the mouth's landmark; if it is flat or symmetrical, the mouth reads as artificial. One side of the bow is usually slightly higher than the other, even in a neutral face.
  • The vermilion border — the edge where the lip meets the skin. It is a thin dark line, and it is thinner than beginners draw. At portrait scale it is often a value transition, not a visible line.
  • The mouth corner — the darkest point of the whole mouth. The skin folds there, so it sits in shadow. It is the anchor that keeps the lips attached to the face.
  • The proportion — the lower lip is thicker than the upper one in most faces, and the mouth sits roughly a third of the way down from the base of the nose to the bottom of the chin.

Spend sixty seconds on a reference photo finding these four points. Everything in the next five steps is built on them.

Step 1 — Draw the line (a value, not an outline)

The mouth's edge is drawn with a brush, not a pen. Use a small, soft-ish brush at medium opacity and paint the border as a thin darkening that varies in weight:

  • Thickest at the corners and along the centre of the upper lip (where the cupid's bow is).
  • Thinnest, almost dissolving, at the outer thirds of the lower lip — the lower lip's edge is softer because the surface curves away.
  • A touch darker than you think it needs to be, because the next steps will soften it — but never black. The line is the darkest value of the mouth region, and it should read as shadow, not ink.

Then the two small lines that do more than they look like: the philtrum — the two faint grooves running from the nose to the upper lip — as a whisper of shadow, and the mouth opening line, the dark crease between the lips where they meet. In a closed mouth the opening line is the second-darkest element after the corners, and it is slightly curved (the lips part in a shallow smile shape even when neutral), never a straight horizontal.

Keep the line on its own layer. You will adjust it in step 5, and you will thank yourself.

Step 2 — Base colour: the lower lip is lighter

Now the pigment. Two facts before the first stroke:

  • Start a step darker and more saturated than the final colour. Blending lightens and softens; you cannot reach back to a deeper tone without covering what you have. Mid-to-deep is the safe starting point, and the final colour will emerge from the blending, not from the base.
  • The lower lip is lighter than the upper. The lower lip faces the light more directly and is thicker, so it carries the mouth's main value. Paint the upper lip a step darker and slightly less saturated than the lower, and the mouth immediately has dimension before you have done any shading.

Paint both lips as soft masses, slightly past the border line — real pigment is softest at the edge, and a hard stop at the line is the fastest way to look drawn. A clean brush at 50–70% opacity, one pass per lip, and stop. You are laying a base, not finishing.

Step 3 — Depth: the shadows that build the form

The lips are a folded tube, and the folds cast the mouth's small shadows. Three of them, in order:

  1. The mouth opening crease. Where the lips meet, the skin folds — a narrow dark line, darkest at the corners, fading at the centre. Reinforce the line from step 1 here with a soft brush at 30–40% opacity, keeping it narrow.
  2. Under the lower lip. The lower lip overhangs the chin, so it casts a soft shadow on the skin below — a gentle darkening that is widest at the corners and fades toward the centre. This shadow does double duty: it builds the lip's volume and anchors the mouth to the chin.
  3. Above the upper lip. A fainter shadow sits on the skin between the upper lip and the nose, under the philtrum. Keep it very soft — 15–20% — because the skin there is close to the nose and the shadow is shallow.

All three on their own layer (or a clipped layer above the lip base), all soft, none of them darker than the mouth line. The rule for small features: the shadows are soft and close to the base value — hard, deep shadows on the mouth read as damage, not form.

Step 4 — Volume and shine: the light that makes them 3D

Now the light side, in the same order you would find it on a reference:

  1. The lower-lip highlight. The centre of the lower lip, slightly below its midline, is where the curved surface faces the light. Lighten a small horizontal area there — base plus light, still carrying hue, never white. It is the mouth's main highlight, and its size should be modest: a highlight bigger than the central third of the lower lip starts to look glossy in a way that is not in the reference.
  2. The cupid's-bow light. A faint secondary highlight along the top edge of the bow, where the ridge catches the light. One thin pass, low opacity. It is the detail that makes the bow read as a ridge instead of a line.
  3. The corner fade. The corners are the darkest points — deepen them a touch with the mouth-line colour, then let the lip pigment fade into them so the colour does not stop abruptly at the corner.

Matte versus glossy is a decision at this step, and it is a value decision, not an effects decision:

Matte lipsGlossy lips
SaturationEven across the surfaceSlightly reduced toward the centre
Lower-lip lightSoft, wide lightening, no crisp edgeBrighter, smaller, with a defined centre
HighlightNone — just the soft lighteningOne small, crisp highlight at the centre
Edge qualitySofter, more pigment bleed past the lineSlightly cleaner edge, colour holds to the line

Whichever you choose, commit: the most common beginner result is a lip that is half-matte and half-glossy because the artist added a "just a little shine" to an already-soft base. Decide from the reference, then execute the row above.

Step 5 — Blend into the face (and the final check)

Lips do not exist in isolation — the surrounding skin takes on the lips' colour a little, and the mouth's shadows connect to the face's shadow system. Three finishing moves:

  • Bleed the colour out, not in. A very low-opacity pass (10–15%) of the lip colour on the skin immediately outside the border — the "bleed" of pigment that real lips have. It is subtle; if you can see it at full zoom, it is too strong, but at working size it is what makes the lip look grown rather than drawn.
  • Check the shadow chain. The under-lip shadow should connect visually to the under-chin shadow, and the corner shadows should sit inside the face's overall shadow logic. If the mouth's shadows point one way and the nose's point another, one of them is wrong — the lighting guide is the reference for which.
  • Quick pinch, judge at size. Zoom out to the size the finished portrait will be viewed. If the mouth reads — shape, a dark corner, a light lower centre — it is done. Details that only exist at 400% zoom are details you will remove on the next pass, so leave them out.

The five mistakes that make lips look painted

  • A hard black outline. The border is a value, thin and soft. If it reads as a line before it reads as a lip, it is too dark or too crisp.
  • Uniform colour, upper and lower. The lower lip is lighter. If both halves are the same value, the mouth is a flat band — add the step difference and the form appears.
  • The highlight in the wrong place. The main highlight belongs on the lower lip, centre, slightly low. A highlight on the upper lip body is the most common tell of an unobserved mouth.
  • Ignored corners. A mouth whose corners are the same value as its centre floats. Darken the corners a step, and the mouth anchors.
  • A lip colour that ignores the skin. The lips share the face's undertone — warm skin wants a warm-leaning lip, cool skin a cooler one. The skin tone mixing guide covers the undertone logic; the lips are just the most saturated expression of it.

What to do next

Practise the five steps on three references before trusting them in a finished portrait — a front view, a three-quarter view, and one slightly smiling mouth. The third one is where the mouth-corner logic really kicks in, and it is where most of the learning happens.
