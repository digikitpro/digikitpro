Procreate Blend Modes Explained: A Visual Guide for Beginners

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Open the blend mode list in Procreate and you see about twenty-five options, most of them with names that sound like a physics exam. Here is the honest summary: you will use six of them, and four of those six will do ninety percent of the work. The rest exist for specific effects, and this article tells you what each one does in plain language, with one practical use you can apply today.

A blend mode controls how the pixels on a layer combine with the pixels on the layers beneath it. The default — Normal — means "sit on top, opaque where I have paint, transparent where I do not." Every other mode is a different rule for combining, and once you understand the rules, the list stops being intimidating and starts being a toolbox.

How to change the blend mode (and preview it live)

Open the Layers panel (the double-rectangle icon in the top toolbar). Each layer shows a small letter on its right — the active mode's abbreviation: N for Normal, M for Multiply, S for Screen, and so on. Tap the letter and a scrolling list opens, and here is the useful part: as you scroll, Procreate applies each mode to your layer live. You are watching your actual painting transform through the whole list. That live preview is the fastest way to learn the modes — scroll it once over a busy layer and you will remember more than from reading any chart.

Under the mode name in that same menu is the layer's opacity slider, and the two work together: most blend modes are meant to be used at reduced opacity, and the two-finger tap on the layer gives you a fast opacity drag without leaving the canvas.

The six modes you will actually use

Multiply (M) — the shadow workhorse

Multiply multiplies the lightness of your layer into the layer below. The practical consequence: white is invisible, and everything else darkens the base. Paint a shadow in any colour on a layer set to Multiply and the white parts of your layer vanish — only the darkening remains, mixed with whatever is underneath.

Use it for: soft shadows (a charcoal or grey brush, low opacity), line art placed over a painted base (the lines darken the colour instead of sitting on it as ink), and texture overlays (a paper-grain layer at 10–20% Multiply adds tooth without changing your colours). It is the first mode to learn and the one that stays in daily use forever.

Screen (S) — the light workhorse

Screen is Multiply's mirror: black is invisible, and everything else lightens the base. Paint a glow, a highlight, or a spark in white or pale colour on a Screen layer and the dark parts disappear — only the lightening remains.

Use it for: glows and light effects (a soft white radial on a Screen layer at 30–50% is the classic rim-light and halo), glitter and sparkle passes (the Sparkle & Shine set is designed to run on Screen, where the black between the sparkles vanishes and only the glints remain), and lifting a shadow area with a touch of light. If Multiply is how you add darkness, Screen is how you add light without painting over anything.

Overlay (O) — the contrast punch

Overlay combines Multiply and Screen: dark areas on your layer darken the base, light areas lighten it, and the effect is strongest in the midtones. It is a contrast amplifier — anything with midtone detail in it gains punch when overlaid.

Use it for: a texture layer (paper grain, a scanned canvas) at 20–40% that makes the whole painting feel printed on a real surface; a duplicate of your painting, slightly blurred or colour-shifted, for a graded look; and a midtone-contrast pass on a flat painting. Overlay at 100% is loud — it is a mode you almost always turn down.

Soft Light (SL) — the gentle version of the same idea

Soft Light does what Overlay does, gently: dark areas darken slightly, light areas lighten slightly, mid-greys barely move at all. It is the finishing-pass mode — the one that says "this is almost right, just let it breathe."

Use it for: a final unifying pass (a light warm grey over the whole painting at 15–25% Soft Light ties colours together), a very soft texture, and toning down a painting that is a touch harsh. If Overlay is a contrast filter, Soft Light is a toning cloth.

Color (C) — recolour, keep the shading

Color takes the hue and saturation of your layer and applies them to the base, while keeping the base's values (its lights and darks) intact. Paint a flat shape in any colour on a Color layer over a fully shaded form, and the shape takes the form's shading while wearing your colour.

Use it for: recolouring a completed piece (paint a new base colour on a Color layer over a value study — the study's shading survives, only the colour changes), consistent colour across a character's repeated outfits or props, and a quick "what if this were blue?" test that costs one layer and zero repainting.

Normal (N) — the mode you already use

Worth listing because it is the reference point: opaque where painted, transparent elsewhere, no interaction with the layers below. Most of your painting lives in Normal, and the other modes are effects you add around it.

The rest of the list, at a glance

You will meet these less often. One line each, and when they earn their place:

Notice the pattern in the list: everything pairs up (darken/lighten, burn/dodge, multiply/screen, hue/saturation/luminosity), and the pairs are mirrors of each other. Learn one side of a pair and you have already learned the other.

The opacity multiplier: the trick behind most "blend mode effects"

Most of the time a blend mode looks wrong, the mode is right and the opacity is not. The working defaults:

  • Multiply shadows: 30–60% for soft shape shadows, 10–20% for texture.
  • Screen glows: 30–50% for light, 20–40% for glitter passes.
  • Overlay texture: 20–40%, never 100% unless you want it obvious.
  • Soft Light finishing: 15–30%.
  • Color / Hue / Luminosity: often 100%, because they are recolouring tools, not effects.

Two-finger tap the layer, drag the opacity until the effect sits at the back of your eye instead of the front of it, and that is your setting for the next time. Blend mode work is judgement work — the numbers are a starting grid, not a law.

Blend modes versus blending brushes (do not confuse the two)

Procreate has two "blending" systems, and beginners mix them up constantly:

  • A blend mode is a per-layer rule. It applies to the whole layer at once — how that layer's pixels combine with everything below. It is set in the Layers panel, and it is non-destructive: change the mode or opacity and the underlying painting is untouched.
  • A blending brush (smudge, oil, watercolour blender) is a per-stroke tool. It drags and mixes the pixels it touches, on the layer you are painting, as you paint. It is destructive — the strokes you lay down are committed.

The two are complementary, not competing: a smudge brush blends within a layer (softening a shadow edge, working a highlight into a midtone), while a blend mode blends between layers (a texture over a painting, a glow over a face). Our blending brushes guide covers the brush side in depth — opacity, pass count, and how to avoid the airbrushed look — and this article is the layer side of the same skill.

And both sit on top of the layer system itself: clipping masks keep your shading inside a shape, Alpha Lock keeps it inside a painted area, and blend modes decide how the result interacts with what is underneath. The layers explained guide is the foundation for all three.

A ten-minute exercise to feel the difference

Make a simple painting — a sphere on a grey background, or a face thumbnail, anything with light and shadow. Then, in order, duplicate the top layer and set each duplicate to: Multiply at 50%, Screen at 50%, Overlay at 40%, Soft Light at 40%, Color (paint it a flat blue first). Look at the five results side by side. You have now seen the entire working vocabulary of blend modes in ten minutes, on your own painting, and you will remember it because you saw it happen.

What to do next

Two workflows where blend modes pay off immediately:

  • The texture pass. A paper-grain or canvas layer at 15–25% Multiply over a finished painting is the single fastest "make it feel real" move in Procreate, and it costs one layer. The full set of texture applications — including the Screen-mode glow passes and the Soft Light unifying pass — is in the texture techniques guide.
  • The portrait finish. A Screen layer for the rim light, a Multiply layer for the final shadow deepening, a Soft Light pass to unify — the Portrait Mastery Kit is organised so the finishing brushes (texture and finish collections) sit exactly where this sequence needs them, and its structure mirrors the layer plan above.

Learn the six core modes this week — Multiply, Screen, Overlay, Soft Light, Color, and Normal. The other nineteen will introduce themselves when you need them.
