Procreate Texture Techniques: 7 Ways to Add Depth to Your Art

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 20 September 2026

Digital art has a characteristic failure, and it is the opposite of what beginners expect. Traditional art fails by being too rough — muddy, uneven, hard to control. Digital art fails by being too clean: every edge crisp, every surface even, every gradient smooth. The viewer does not say "this looks digital." They say "this looks finished," and something in them knows that real marks are messier than this.

Texture is the bridge. Not noise slapped over everything — that is its own failure mode — but deliberate texture: paper where the paper should be, broken edges where a real brush would break, visible marks where a real hand left them. This article is seven specific techniques, each with the layer setup and the opacity that makes it work, and it ends with the one rule that separates texture from noise.

The setup all seven techniques share

Before the techniques, the structure. Every texture in this article lives on its own layer, above the painting it is texturing, at low opacity, and in a blend mode chosen for the job. Three consequences:

  • Non-destructive. The texture is a layer, not a change. Too strong? Lower the opacity. Wrong place? Paint it out or erase it. The painting underneath is untouched.
  • Adjustable per zone. With a selection or a mask you can keep texture off the faces and out of the fine detail — the most common "too gritty" failure is texture over detail.
  • Reusable. A good texture layer can be duplicated across projects. Build a small personal library of five or ten texture layers and your paintings start to share a surface, which is exactly what a consistent artist's body of work should do.

The blend mode defaults, for reference: Multiply (10–25%) for grain and darkening textures, Soft Light (15–30%) for grain that should add light as well as dark, Screen (20–40%) for light-based effects like dust motes and glow, and Normal (10–30%) for painted-on effects like splatter that should read as actual marks. The blend modes guide explains how each one interacts with the layers below if you want the full logic.

Technique 1 — Paper grain as the foundation

The single highest-value texture in Procreate: a full-canvas paper grain, laid down early, that everything else paints over or sits on top of.

  • Setup: a layer below your first painted layer (or above everything for a final pass — both work; below is more integrated, above is more uniform). Fill it with a paper-grain brush or import a scanned paper texture. Multiply, 10–20%.
  • What it does: gives every surface a shared tooth. Painted areas pick up the grain through their edges, flat areas carry it directly, and the whole piece starts reading as "painted on a surface" instead of "painted on nothing."
  • The check: zoom to 100% and look at a flat midtone. You should see the grain, but only when you look for it. If it jumps at arm's length, halve the opacity.

A dedicated paper-texture brush set does this in one stroke — the Free Fine Liner Set, for example, includes 13 paper textures alongside its liners, which makes it a good zero-cost source of foundation grain.

Technique 2 — Broken, rough edges

Clean edges are the other half of the digital tell. A shape with a perfectly even outline everywhere reads as vector, even when the interior is full of painterly texture. Real marks break at the edges — paint thins out, bristles skip, charcoal crumbs.

  • Setup: after a shape is blocked in with a clean brush, take a dry textured brush (a rough watercolour edge, a dry charcoal, a spatter-fine) and run it lightly along the main edges, at 30–50% opacity on the same layer or a clipped layer above.
  • What it does: the edge stops being a line and becomes a transition with accidents in it. The shape is still there — the outline is just no longer perfect, which is the difference between "drawn" and "made."
  • Where it matters most: the big shapes — a hair mass, a background block, a garment silhouette. Small shapes can afford clean edges; the large ones cannot.
  • What to avoid: doing this on line art you want to stay crisp. Broken edges are a painter's move; a crisp inking style has its own logic, and mixing the two is how pieces start to look undecided.

Technique 3 — Dry-brush marks

Dry-brushing is the technique that most directly imitates a real brush running out of paint: a stroke that carries pigment in the centre of the bristle and nothing at the edges, so the underlying surface shows through in streaks.

  • Setup: a brush with low ink flow and a textured tip — most watercolour and charcoal brushes have a dry variant, or lower the ink flow of a textured brush until the stroke starts to break up. Paint at a light pressure and a faster speed than your normal work; both increase the break-up.
  • What it does: adds directional marks that read as brushwork. A dry-brush pass over a shaded area turns a smooth gradient into a surface with history — you can see the strokes that built it.
  • Where it works best: large midtone areas (a sky, a garment, a wall), and the transitions out of shadows, where a few dry streaks do more for the "painted" feel than any amount of blending.
  • The restraint rule: one or two areas per piece. Dry-brushing everything makes the piece look like a demonstration of the technique, which is not what you want.

Technique 4 — Splatter and spatter

Splatter is texture as event: a moment where pigment flew, and the record of it stays. Used well it adds energy and a human hand; used badly it adds dust.

  • Setup: a splatter stamp or spatter brush (stamps are faster and more controllable; the stamp versus painting brush guide covers when each type earns its place). Multiply, 20–50%, on its own layer above the area being splattered.
  • What it does: breaks large flat areas, suggests motion, and — placed near a light source or a focal point — directs the eye. A few spatter marks trailing away from a bright area read as sparks; the same marks scattered evenly read as dirt.
  • The placement rule: splatter should have a direction and a density gradient — dense near the source, sparse away from it. Even distribution is the failure mode, because real splatter is a physics event and physics is never even.
  • Negative space: the space between the spatter marks is part of the effect. If the layer looks busy at working size, delete half the marks — the effect survives, the dust does not.

Technique 5 — The grain overlay: the final unifying pass

The last technique is the most used in finished work: a full-canvas grain laid over everything, at the very end, in one pass.

  • Setup: a fine noise or film-grain layer above all layers. Multiply at 5–15%, or Soft Light at 10–20% for a grain that brightens as well as darkens. One layer, one setting, done.
  • What it does: it ties the piece together. Every layer of a digital painting has its own micro-smoothness — the blended skin, the flat background, the crisp line art — and a final grain gives them all the same surface at the same scale, so the eye reads them as one object. It is the digital equivalent of varnish: invisible in the moment, obvious in the difference when it is absent.
  • The check: compare the finished piece with the grain layer hidden. The grained version should feel "one step more finished" without you being able to say exactly what changed. If you can say what changed, the grain is too strong.

Technique 6 — Simulating the medium you are imitating

When a piece is meant to look like a specific traditional medium, the texture work is not generic grain — it is the specific behaviour of that medium, and the technique is learning which behaviour carries the illusion:

  • Charcoal: the carry is the crumb and the smudge — a grainy dry stroke that breaks up, and a directional smudge drag that leaves streaks. The Professional Charcoal Studio set is built around exactly these two behaviours, and a charcoal piece without the smudge streaks reads as graphite-with-a-texture-brush.
  • Watercolour: the carry is the edge and the bloom — pigment pooling at the edge of a wash and the hard granulation where water sits. The realistic watercolour guide walks through building both with layer and opacity control.
  • Oil or acrylic: the carry is the load — thick, directional strokes with visible body, and a glaze pass on top at low opacity. Dry-brush technique (technique 3) is the main tool, with a Soft Light glaze layer for the finish.

The general principle: pick the one behaviour that most defines the medium and commit to it across the whole piece. A piece that smudges like charcoal in one area and blooms like watercolour in another is not a hybrid — it is confused.

Technique 7 — Selective texture (the restraint technique)

The last technique is a technique of omission, and it is the one that separates texture from noise:

  • The rule: texture belongs to some surfaces, not all. In a portrait, the skin gets pores and a faint grain, the hair gets strand texture, the background gets paper — but the eyes, the lips and the main highlights stay clean. The clean zones are what the textured zones are measured against, and if everything is textured, nothing is.
  • The method: before adding any texture, mark the two or three zones that must stay clean (usually the focal point and its immediate surroundings). Build the texture layers with those zones excluded — a mask, a careful edge, or simply stopping the strokes short. The contrast between the textured field and the clean focal area is the depth.
  • The check: cover the textured zones and look at the clean ones. They should read as "deliberately smooth," not "unfinished." If they read as unfinished, you have under-textured the rest of the piece — go back and build the field up to meet them.

This is the technique the digital-art-looks-traditional guide builds its whole argument on: the traditional look is not the presence of texture, it is the pattern of texture — where the marks are, where they break, and where the surface is allowed to be smooth.

The one rule of texture

Seven techniques, one rule: texture should be visible when you look for it, and invisible when you look at the piece. If a viewer can point at the texture and name it, it is a technique. If they only feel that the piece has a surface, it is texture. Every opacity in this article is set to land on the invisible side of that line — and the grain overlay check (hide the layer, compare) is the test that tells you which side you are on.

What to do next

Try the two that pay off fastest on your next piece: the paper grain foundation (technique 1) and the final grain overlay (technique 5). Together they cost you two layers and ten minutes, and they will change how the whole piece reads before you touch a single other technique.

For brushes built around the specific behaviours above: the Professional Charcoal Studio carries the crumb-and-smudge pair for charcoal work, the Premium Texture Effects set is a small, focused collection of the finishing-pass textures (grain, splatter, edge) for pieces that need the final third of the effect, and the free Chalkboard Artist's Toolkit is the zero-cost entry point — its chalk brushes demonstrate the dry-mark behaviour (technique 3) better than almost anything else in the default library.
