The Complete Procreate Portrait Workflow: Sketch to Final Polish

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 2026-09-14

A portrait becomes easier to finish when each stage has one job. The sketch checks proportion. The value pass makes the head three-dimensional. The colour pass establishes temperature. Skin, hair, edges, and polish come later. If you ask a detail brush to solve a construction problem, the file becomes busy without becoming better.

This Procreate portrait workflow is a repeatable sequence for a head study or illustrative portrait. It uses suggested layer names and settings, not fixed rules. Adjust the canvas and brush scale to your output and iPad’s layer budget.

What you need

  • A reference with a clear light direction and a pose you can actually study.
  • Procreate on iPad and an Apple Pencil if you want pressure and tilt response.
  • A sketch brush, painting brush, textured blender, hair tool, and a detail brush.
  • A working document with enough layers for your process. Procreate’s custom canvas guide notes that larger canvases offer fewer maximum layers.

Step 1: Choose the reference and plan the crop

Pick a reference that gives you a readable head angle, simple enough lighting, and a crop that suits your goal. Decide whether the portrait is a study, profile image, print, or character illustration before you start detailing.

Make a small thumbnail. Check the position of the head, background shape, and brightest light. If the composition works small, the painting has somewhere to go.

Step 2: Create a practical canvas and layer stack

Create the document for the final destination. For print, calculate the physical size at the requested DPI; for screen, prioritise pixel dimensions and the platform requirements. A suggested layer stack is:

  • Reference / notes
  • Construction
  • Sketch
  • Flat colour
  • Form shadows
  • Colour variation
  • Skin texture
  • Hair
  • Details
  • Background / finish

You do not need every layer in every portrait. The point is to keep decisions reversible until the large forms work.

Step 3: Build the construction

Place the head shape, centre line, brow line, eye sockets, nose base, mouth, jaw, ears, neck, and hairline. Compare angles and distances rather than drawing features as symbols. Keep the sketch lighter than the final line or paint.

Flip the canvas and zoom out. Fix proportion now; it is cheaper than correcting it after texture.

Step 4: Establish the value groups

Fill the local colour simply, then add the major light and shadow families. Ask whether the portrait reads without eyelashes, pores, hair strands, or bright highlights. If it does not, simplify the forms and improve the value structure.

Use a large brush and suggested low-to-medium opacity so you can build the shadow gradually. Keep reflected light subordinate to the main light.

Step 5: Add colour temperature and surface planes

Introduce warm and cool shifts based on the reference and environment. Keep the changes connected to the form: cheeks, nose, ears, lips, and reflected light may behave differently from the forehead or shadow side. Do not apply a generic skin recipe to every complexion.

The Portrait Skin Brushes product is catalogued with base, blusher, highlighter, textured blend, and skin-detail tools. Use texture only after the value and colour planes are stable.

Step 6: Paint the hair as a mass

Block the hair silhouette and large clumps before strands. The Hair & Hairstyle Stamp Kit can help explore a hairstyle foundation, while a painting brush is better for adapting the flow and finishing the surface. See the dedicated hair tutorial for the mass-to-strand process.

Step 7: Refine features and edges

Use the hardest edges where information matters: selected eyelids, the pupil, a lip edge, a nostril opening, or a small highlight. Soften the cheek, jaw, neck, and background transitions where the form turns away.

Eyes deserve attention, but do not render them at full detail while the head is still unresolved. Return to the thumbnail repeatedly.

Step 8: Add texture and finish

Place pores, freckles, hair strands, paper grain, or small accents at the end and at controlled strength. Hide the texture layer to make sure the painting still works. Procreate’s Blend Modes reference explains the available layer interactions; use them as optional tools rather than automatic fixes.

Step 9: Review and export

Flip the canvas, check the silhouette, inspect the values in grayscale, and compare the finished work to the reference. Remove details that compete with the focal point. Save the layered working file, then export the format required by the final destination.

FAQ

What is the best order for painting a portrait in Procreate?

Use reference and crop planning, canvas and layers, construction, values, colour, hair mass, features and edges, texture, then final review. The exact order can flex, but large structure should come before surface detail.

How many layers do I need for a Procreate portrait?

There is no fixed number. Use enough to keep the major decisions reversible without making the file difficult to manage. Larger canvases may reduce the maximum layer count; Procreate shows that limit when you create or edit the canvas.

Should I paint skin or hair first?

Establish the head and major hair silhouette early so the composition and background values are clear. Detailed skin and hair rendering can happen after the large value structure works.

Can I follow this workflow with Procreate’s built-in brushes?

Yes. A purchased kit can organise specialist marks, but the workflow depends on drawing, values, colour, and edges rather than any single product.

Is a portrait brush bundle worth it?

It is worth considering when you use its included stages. The Ultimate Portrait Mastery Bundle is relevant for artists who need line art, skin, hair-stamp, and finishing resources; a focused kit is better if you only need one stage.
