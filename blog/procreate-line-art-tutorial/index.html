Procreate Line Art Tutorial: How to Draw Clean Lines and Confident Ink

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 19 September 2026

Clean line art is not about having a steady hand. It is about understanding how a line communicates — where it should be thick, where it should taper, and when a confident single stroke reads better than three hesitant ones.

This tutorial covers the full inking process in Procreate: choosing a brush, setting up your layers, controlling line weight, using stabilisation, and finishing a clean piece from a rough sketch. The techniques work whether you are drawing illustration, manga, character design, or portrait line work.

Step 1: Choose a liner brush that responds to pressure

A good inking brush does two things: it produces a consistent line at a given pressure, and it tapers at the start and end of a stroke. Most of Procreate's built-in inking brushes handle this well — the Studio Pen and Technical Pen in the Inking category are reliable defaults.

If you want a dedicated line art toolkit, the Free Fine Liner Set includes 100 brushes covering fine liners, microns, and markers. You do not need all of them: pick one that tapers nicely and one that holds a consistent width, then ignore the rest until you have a reason to explore further.

Before you start inking, test your brush on a blank area:

  • Draw a slow, curved line. Does the width change smoothly with pressure?
  • Draw a fast flick. Does the end taper cleanly, or does it leave a blob?
  • Draw overlapping lines. Do they build up dark where they cross, or does the brush ignore overlap?

Understanding your brush's behaviour before you commit to a drawing saves frustration later.

Step 2: Set up your layers for inking

Never ink on the same layer as your sketch. The standard setup is:

  1. Sketch layer — your rough construction drawing. Lower its opacity to 20–30% so you can see it faintly without it interfering with your ink.
  2. Ink layer — a new layer above the sketch. This is where your final line art goes.
  3. Colour layer — a layer below the ink for flat colours and shading.

If your line art is complex (a character with detailed clothing, for example), consider splitting the ink layer into foreground lines and background lines on separate layers. This lets you adjust, recolour, or delete one set without affecting the other.

Our layers explained guide covers the full layer system if you want to understand Clipping Masks, Alpha Lock, and layer organisation.

Step 3: Use stabilisation for smoother strokes

If your lines look shaky or jagged, Procreate's built-in stabilisation can smooth them. Tap your brush to open Brush Studio, then go to the Stabilization section. Two settings matter most:

  • StreamLine — smooths the path of your stroke, reducing wobble. Start at 15–25% and increase if needed.
  • Motion Filtering — averages the input to remove small jitters. Useful for very slow, controlled lines.

Do not set stabilisation to maximum. Too much smoothing introduces lag — the brush trails behind your pencil — and makes fast strokes feel unresponsive. Find the lowest amount that removes the visible shakiness and leave it there.

You can also enable stabilisation at the canvas level: tap the Actions menu (wrench) → Prefs → Gesture Controls → Stabilization. This applies to all brushes without editing them individually.

Step 4: Draw with your arm, not your wrist

This is the physical technique behind confident lines. Short wrist-driven strokes are fine for small details, but long curves and outlines should come from the elbow and shoulder. This produces smoother, more fluid lines because larger joints move with less micro-tremor.

Practise with warm-up exercises before you start inking:

  1. Long curves. Draw a series of long C-shapes and S-curves across the canvas. Focus on smooth, single-pass strokes.
  2. Parallel lines. Fill an area with evenly spaced parallel lines. This builds control over spacing and consistency.
  3. Ellipses. Draw a row of ellipses at different angles. Ellipses reveal wobble more clearly than any other shape.

Five minutes of warm-up lines before an inking session makes a visible difference in the quality of the final work.

Step 5: Understand line weight

Line weight is the variation in line thickness across a drawing. Uniform-weight lines look flat and mechanical. Varied lines create depth, weight, and visual interest.

Here are the basic rules for where to use thick and thin lines:

Thicker lines

  • Shadow areas. Lines on the shadow side of a form are heavier. The underside of an arm, the area under the chin, the side of a face away from the light.
  • Foreground elements. Objects closer to the viewer have heavier outlines. Objects in the background have thinner, lighter lines.
  • Weight-bearing structures. The base of a column, the bottom of a character's body, the ground line — thicker lines suggest physical weight.
  • Overlapping edges. Where one object passes in front of another, the overlapping line is thicker to clarify depth.

Thinner lines

  • Light-facing areas. Lines on the lit side of a form are thinner or sometimes absent entirely.
  • Interior details. Facial features, fabric folds, and surface patterns use thinner lines than the outer contour.
  • Background elements. Distant objects, background characters, and environmental details use lighter, thinner lines.
  • Highlights. Where light hits directly, lines can thin to nothing — the highlight "eats" the line.

Line weight in practice

The simplest way to practise line weight is to do a two-pass inking process:

  1. First pass: Draw all lines at a consistent medium weight. Focus on getting the shapes right.
  2. Second pass: Go back and thicken the lines that should be heavier. Use more pressure on the shadow side and less on the light side.

With practice, you will start making these variations in a single pass. The Essential Line Art & Sketch Kit includes 43 brushes organised by role — sketch, ink, detail — and the pressure-responsive liners respond well to deliberate weight changes.

Step 6: Use QuickShape for precise geometry

Procreate's QuickShape feature snaps hand-drawn shapes into clean geometric forms. Draw a shape (circle, rectangle, triangle, line) and hold your pencil on the screen at the end of the stroke. After a brief pause, the shape snaps into its perfect version.

QuickShape is useful for:

  • Architectural elements — windows, doors, building edges
  • Mechanical objects — vehicles, gadgets, weapons
  • Frames, panels, and borders
  • Any straight line that needs to be perfectly straight

To enable or adjust QuickShape, go to Actions → Prefs → Gesture Controls → QuickShape. You can set the hold duration and whether shapes snap to specific angles.

Step 7: Ink confidently, then clean up

A confident wrong line is easier to fix than a hesitant right one. When inking, commit to each stroke. If a line is wrong, undo it with a two-finger tap and redraw it — do not try to erase or patch it. Redrawing builds muscle memory and produces cleaner results than corrective erasing.

After the main inking pass, do a cleanup sweep:

  1. Zoom in to 100% and look for stray marks outside the intended areas.
  2. Check line intersections — do they meet cleanly, or are there overshoots and gaps?
  3. Look at line weight consistency — are the shadow lines clearly thicker than the light-side lines?
  4. Erase with the same brush you inked with (tap and hold the eraser, then select your inking brush) so the eraser edges match the ink edges.

Step 8: Colour under the line art

Once the ink layer is finished, create colour layers below it. Set the line art layer's blend mode to Multiply if you want colours to show through the lines naturally — this is the standard approach for coloured illustration.

To fill flat colours quickly, use the ColourDrop method: drag a colour from the colour circle into a closed area of the line art. Hold briefly and slide left or right to adjust the ColourDrop threshold until the fill reaches the lines without bleeding through. Set the line art layer as a Reference layer so fills on the colour layer below respect the line boundaries.

Line art exercises for practice

These three exercises build specific skills. Do each one for 10–15 minutes:

Contour drawing

Find a simple object (a mug, a shoe, a plant) and draw its outline in a single continuous line without looking at your iPad. The goal is not accuracy — it is hand-eye coordination and learning to trust long, unbroken strokes.

Line weight study

Take an existing sketch or a reference photo and ink it twice: once with a single uniform weight, and once with varied line weight following the rules above. Compare the two results side by side. The difference in depth and readability will be immediate.

Speed inking

Set a timer for five minutes and ink a simple subject — a face, a hand, a piece of fruit — without using undo. This trains you to commit to strokes and accept imperfection. Speed inking is not about producing gallery-ready work; it is about breaking the habit of hesitant, over-corrected lines.
