10 Procreate Mistakes Every Beginner Makes (and How to Fix Them)

By the DigiKitPro studio · Published 19 September 2026

Every new Procreate user makes the same handful of mistakes. Not because the app is difficult — it is one of the most approachable drawing tools available — but because its simplicity hides features that only matter once you know they exist.

This article lists ten problems that show up repeatedly in beginner forums, course Q&As, and studio feedback. Each one comes with a concrete fix you can apply in your next session. None of them require buying anything. Most of them take less than two minutes to correct.

1. Starting on a canvas that is too large

The instinct when opening Procreate for the first time is to create the biggest canvas your iPad allows. More space feels like more freedom. In practice, a large canvas causes three problems:

  • Procreate reduces the number of available layers as canvas size increases.
  • Brushes respond more slowly on very large canvases, especially on older iPads.
  • Texture brushes lose their intended scale — a paper grain that looks fine on a 2000-pixel canvas looks like noise on a 6000-pixel one.

The fix: For practice, studies, and learning exercises, use a 2000×2500 pixel canvas at 300 DPI. That is enough resolution for printing an A5 study while keeping your layer count and brush performance intact. Save the 4K canvases for finished pieces you plan to sell as prints. Our Procreate canvas size and DPI guide covers the numbers in more detail.

2. Drawing everything on a single layer

This is the most common first-week mistake. A single-layer drawing works until you need to change something — and then every edit affects every other element. Changing the background colour smears into the character. Erasing a stray line removes part of the shading underneath.

The fix: Use at least four layers from the start:

  1. Sketch — construction lines and guides (low opacity, hide when finished).
  2. Line art — your final inked or pencilled lines.
  3. Base colour — flat colours, placed below the line art.
  4. Shading and highlights — on separate layers using clipping masks to stay within the base colour.

Layers cost nothing and save hours. Procreate's official layers documentation explains the full layer panel if you want to go deeper.

3. Ignoring Alpha Lock and Clipping Masks

Many beginners manually erase around the edges of shapes to keep shading neat. That approach is slow, destructive, and difficult to correct later.

Procreate offers two tools designed to solve exactly this problem:

  • Alpha Lock locks the transparent pixels on a layer so you can only paint where colour already exists. Activate it by swiping right with two fingers on the layer thumbnail. Use it for quick shading on the same layer as your base colour.
  • Clipping Mask does the same thing but on a separate layer above your base. Create a new layer, tap it, and select Clipping Mask. Everything you paint on that layer is visible only where the layer below has pixels.

The fix: Use Alpha Lock for fast single-layer edits and Clipping Masks when you want to keep your shading editable on a separate layer. Our full layers explained guide covers when to use each one, with visual examples.

4. Over-blending skin and surfaces

The smudge tool in Procreate is powerful. Beginners often use it at full opacity, dragging it back and forth until every surface looks smooth and airbrushed. The result is the flat, plastic look that makes digital portraits immediately recognisable.

The fix: Set your smudge brush opacity to 25–35% and limit yourself to two or three passes per area. Leave some visible brush texture in the midtones. Add skin texture — pores, freckles, fine lines — on a separate layer after blending. The realistic skin texture guide walks through this process step by step, and the Portrait Skin Brushes kit includes dedicated texture tools for pores, wrinkles, and freckles.

5. Collecting brushes instead of learning them

It is tempting to download every free brush set you find. Within a week your library has 400 brushes and you use six of them. The problem is not the brushes — it is that you never spent enough time with any single one to understand how it responds to pressure, speed, and tilt.

The fix: Pick five brushes — a sketch brush, a liner, a painting brush, a blender, and a texture brush — and use only those for two weeks. Pin them in the Recent section of the Brush Library so you do not have to scroll past the rest. When you can explain what each brush does and when you reach for it, you are ready to add another.

The Free Fine Liner Set is a sensible starting point: it covers sketching, inking, and pen work at zero cost, and you do not need all 100 brushes to get value from it.

6. Using the wrong eraser

Most beginners do not realise that Procreate's eraser can use any brush in your library. The default eraser is a hard-edged circle that leaves visible cut marks against textured strokes. If you painted with a chalk brush and then erased with the default, the eraser edge will not match the painted edge.

The fix: Tap and hold the eraser icon, then select the same brush (or a similar textured brush) that you used to paint. Your eraser strokes will blend naturally into the surrounding marks. This is especially important for charcoal, watercolour, and pencil work where edge quality matters.

7. Not using the two-finger undo gesture

Procreate's undo history is generous — up to 250 steps by default — but many beginners still reach for the undo arrow in the top bar, which breaks their drawing flow. The two-finger tap is faster and keeps your pencil hand on the screen.

The fix: Practise the gesture until it is automatic:

  • Two-finger tap — undo
  • Three-finger tap — redo
  • Two-finger tap and hold — continuous undo

These gestures are listed in Procreate's gestures documentation. Our shortcuts and gestures guide collects the most useful ones in a single reference.

8. Forgetting to name and group layers

A ten-layer drawing is manageable. A forty-layer drawing with every layer called "Layer 12" is not. When you need to find the layer with the left eye highlight at 2 a.m., you will regret not naming it.

The fix: Name layers as you create them. Double-tap a layer thumbnail to rename it. Group related layers by selecting the top layer, swiping right on the ones below it, and tapping Group. A typical portrait might have groups for Sketch, Line Art, Skin, Hair, Eyes, and Background.

9. Not backing up your work

Procreate stores files locally on your iPad. If the app crashes, the iPad is lost, or an iOS update goes wrong, your work goes with it. Procreate does not have built-in cloud sync.

The fix: Export your work regularly. Go to Actions (the wrench icon) → Share → choose a format (Procreate file preserves layers; PSD preserves compatibility with other software). Save to iCloud Drive, Dropbox, or an external drive. For work-in-progress pieces, export once at the end of every session. It takes thirty seconds and prevents catastrophic loss.

10. Comparing your first drawings to finished professional work

This is not a technical mistake, but it is the one that causes the most people to quit. Social media shows finished pieces, not the twenty attempts that came before them. Comparing your week-one portrait to someone's year-ten portfolio is not a useful measurement.

The fix: Compare your work to your own work from last week. Save every finished piece — even the ones you are not happy with — in a dated stack in the Gallery. After a month, scroll through and notice the improvement. A structured practice routine helps more than raw hours: the 30-day Procreate practice plan gives you daily exercises that build specific skills rather than random sketches.

What to do next

Pick one mistake from the list and fix it in your next session. Do not try to correct all ten at once — that is a different kind of overwhelm. Once one habit is in place, the next one becomes easier.
